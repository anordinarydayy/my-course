ACID & the Transaction

Lesson 00 named ACID and let you watch a lost update. By the end you'll know exactly what COMMIT promises, why two of the four guarantees are really dials you tune, and how a crash one millisecond after “success” still leaves your data intact.

00 The primitive

The transaction as the unit of work

A transaction is a group of statements the database agrees to treat as a single, indivisible unit of work. You open one with BEGIN, run any number of statements, and end it two ways: COMMIT makes every change permanent and visible, or ROLLBACK throws all of them away as if none had happened. Between those two words, the whole group either lands together or vanishes together — there is no in-between that the outside world can observe.

sql

BEGIN;
  UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - 60 WHERE id = 1;  -- debit A
  UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance + 60 WHERE id = 2;  -- credit B
COMMIT;  -- both, or (on ROLLBACK) neither

Now the part that trips people up: you are always in a transaction, whether you say so or not. By default Postgres runs in autocommit mode, which means every bare statement you send is silently wrapped in its own one-statement transaction that commits the instant it succeeds. A lone UPDATE is a complete transaction. Typing BEGIN is just how you tell Postgres “don't auto-commit after each statement — hold them open until I say COMMIT,” so that several statements share one all-or-nothing fate.

That sharing is the entire point. A single statement rarely needs the ceremony; the value shows up the moment a real operation is more than one statement and the statements only make sense together:

  • A money transfer is a debit and a credit. Apply one without the other and money is created or destroyed — so they must commit together or not at all.
  • An order is a row in orders and several rows in order_lines. An order with no lines, or lines pointing at an order that never got inserted, is corrupt data.
  • A signup might insert a user, a profile, and a default settings row. Half a signup is a support ticket waiting to happen.

Wrap each of those in BEGIN … COMMIT and you've drawn a boundary around “one logical thing.” Everything else in this lesson — atomicity, consistency, isolation, durability — is a promise the database makes about that boundary. ACID isn't four unrelated features; it's four things a transaction guarantees.

The reframe for this lesson

Don't think of ACID as a checklist of database features. Think of it as the contract of one transaction: given a group of statements between BEGIN and COMMIT, the engine promises they are Atomic (all or none), leave the database Consistent (valid → valid), run in Isolation from others, and — once committed — are Durable. Four adjectives, one noun.

01 The “A”

Atomicity — all or nothing ▸

Atomicity is the guarantee that a transaction is indivisible: every statement inside it takes effect, or — if anything goes wrong — none of them do. The word comes from the Greek for “uncuttable.” The failure mode it defends against is the partial write: the debit that lands while the matching credit is lost, the order header saved without its lines. Atomicity's promise is that such a half-state can never be committed and can never be observed by anyone.

The mechanism is ROLLBACK. While a transaction runs, its changes are provisional — real to that transaction, invisible and undoable to everyone else. If a statement raises an error, if a constraint is violated, or if you simply choose to abort, Postgres unwinds every change back to the state at BEGIN. Notice what you never saw: A at $40 while B was still $100, sitting in the committed database for another transaction to read. That partial state existed only inside the running transaction, and ROLLBACK erased it. This is why atomicity and durability are not in tension — Postgres can be aggressive about not-yet-durable provisional changes precisely because it can always throw them away. The unit of durability is the committed transaction, and a rolled-back one was never committed.

Atomicity is about failure, not concurrency

Keep this letter in its lane. Atomicity answers “what happens when one transaction hits a problem partway through?” — and the answer is always “undo the whole thing.” It says nothing about what other transactions can see while yours runs; that's Isolation (section 03). The lost update from Lesson 00 was an isolation bug, not an atomicity bug.

02 The “C”

Consistency — invariants hold across the boundary

Consistency is the guarantee that a transaction takes the database from one valid state to another valid state — never leaving it in a broken one. “Valid” means every rule you've declared about your data still holds: primary keys are unique, foreign keys point at rows that exist, CHECK constraints pass, NOT NULL columns are filled. If a transaction would violate any of those, the engine refuses the offending statement and — if you don't recover — the transaction can only roll back. Consistency is enforced at the boundary: the checks that matter are the ones true at COMMIT.

The subtle thing about the C is that it's a shared responsibility. Part of it is yours and part is the engine's:

Your half — declare the invariants

  • You decide the rules: balance >= 0, an order total equals the sum of its lines, a status is one of a fixed set.
  • You write them into the schema as constraints — or, where a rule spans rows the engine can't express, into the transaction's logic.
  • If you never declare it, the database can't protect it.

The engine's half — enforce them

  • Every declared constraint is checked as the transaction runs and at commit.
  • A violation raises an error and rolls the transaction back rather than storing bad data.
  • Atomicity is the tool it uses to keep C: undo everything rather than commit something invalid.

So the way to buy consistency is to make the database the keeper of your invariants. Encode them as constraints and they hold no matter which code path, script, or careless human issues the write:

sql

CREATE TABLE accounts (
  id       bigint GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
  owner    text    NOT NULL,
  balance  numeric NOT NULL CHECK (balance >= 0)   -- the invariant, declared once
);

Now any transaction that would push a balance negative fails on that CHECK and rolls back — the debit half of an over-large withdrawal is undone with it. The rule lives in one place, enforced for everyone, forever. (Note the modern choices: GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY instead of serial, and numeric — not float — for money, so cents never drift.)

This C is not the C in CAP

Two famous acronyms, same letter, different meaning — a genuine trap. In ACID, Consistency means “the transaction respects your declared integrity rules.” In the CAP theorem (about distributed systems), Consistency means “every node returns the latest write” — really a statement about replication and reads across machines. They are unrelated concepts that happen to share a word. When someone says a system is “consistent,” ask which C they mean.

03 The “I”

Isolation — the dial ▸

Isolation is the guarantee about concurrency: it governs how much of each other's in-flight work simultaneous transactions can see. The ideal — serializability — is that the result of running many transactions at once is identical to running them one after another in some order. But that ideal costs coordination, so isolation is not a switch; it's a dial, and where you set it trades safety against concurrency. To choose a setting, you have to know the anomalies each level lets through:

  • Dirty read — reading another transaction's uncommitted change (which might still roll back). Postgres never allows this at any level.
  • Non-repeatable read — you read a row, someone else commits an update to it, you read it again and the value changed under you.
  • Phantom — you run a range query (WHERE price < 10), someone commits a new matching row, you re-run and a row appears that wasn't there.
  • Lost update — two transactions read the same value, both write back based on what they read, and one overwrites the other (Lesson 00's bank balance).
  • Write skew — two transactions each read a shared rule, each individually keeps it, but their combined writes break it (both doctors go off-call at once).

PostgreSQL offers three usable levels, and moving up the dial removes anomalies at the price of more conflicts and retries: Read Committed (the default — each statement sees the latest committed data) → Repeatable Read (a snapshot frozen at the transaction's start) → Serializable (as if truly one-at-a-time). The point is not that Read Committed is “wrong” — for most OLTP work it's the right default, because it's cheap and app code rarely reads the same row twice inside one transaction. The point is that the level is a choice with a cost on both sides: turn the dial up and T1 would see a stable 10, but the engine must hold a snapshot and may force a serialization failure that your code has to retry; leave it down and you get maximum concurrency but must design around anomalies like this one. How Postgres implements all of this without locking readers — its multiversion concurrency control, MVCC — is a whole story of its own.

Where this goes next

Isolation is the single richest topic in the course, and it gets an entire phase. 04 The “D”

Durability — surviving a crash ▸

Durability is the guarantee that once COMMIT returns success, the change survives anything short of losing the disk — a process kill, a kernel panic, a yanked power cord. The mechanism is the one Lesson 02 introduced: the write-ahead log (WAL). Before a committed change is considered safe, Postgres writes a compact record of it to the WAL and fsyncs that record to durable storage. Crucially, COMMIT does not return until that fsync completes — so the moment your client hears “committed,” the change is already on disk in the log, even if the data pages themselves are still only in RAM.

Why the log and not the pages? Because appending a small sequential record to one file is far faster than hunting down and flushing every scattered data page a transaction touched. The WAL turns “make it durable” into one cheap sequential write. The data pages catch up lazily later; if a crash beats them to it, the log is enough to reconstruct everything. Recovery has two jobs, and the diagram shows both: redo every change the WAL says was committed (some may not have reached the data files yet), and discard anything that was in flight but never committed. When it finishes, the database is exactly the set of transactions that had heard “committed” — no more, no less. That is durability made mechanical.

And like isolation, durability has a dial. The synchronous_commit setting decides how hard COMMIT waits:

sql

-- the durability dial (settable per session or globally)
SET synchronous_commit = on;   -- default: COMMIT waits for the WAL fsync (safe)
SET synchronous_commit = off;  -- faster; a crash may lose the last ~fraction of a second of commits

With it off, COMMIT returns before the fsync, so a crash can lose the most recent commits — but the database is never corrupted (it still recovers to a consistent point, just an slightly older one). That's a deliberate trade of a little durability for throughput, and it's exactly the kind of decision Phases 7 and 8 make explicit — right alongside replication, where a commit can even wait for a WAL record to reach a second machine before it returns.

Where this goes next

The WAL is the backbone of far more than crash recovery: point-in-time restore, streaming replication, and read replicas are all “replay this log somewhere else.” Phase 7 (Durability, backups & the WAL) and Phase 8 (replication & scale) build on exactly the picture in this diagram. For now: COMMIT means fsync'd to the WAL, and how synchronously it waits is a tunable dial.

05 Reason about it

Using transactions well

Let's turn the four guarantees into habits. Reasoning through how transactions behave in real code is what separates “I know what ACID stands for” from “I can write correct concurrent database code.” Walk through the decisions you'll actually make.

Wrap a logical operation, then commit or roll back. The shape almost every codebase uses is: BEGIN, run the statements, and on success COMMIT; on any exception, ROLLBACK. Most database libraries give you a transaction block that does exactly this — commit if the block returns normally, roll back if it throws — so you rarely type the keywords by hand, but that is precisely what runs underneath.

An error inside a transaction aborts the whole thing. This one surprises newcomers to Postgres: the instant any statement raises an error, the transaction enters an aborted state, and every subsequent statement is rejected with “current transaction is aborted, commands ignored until end of transaction block” — until you issue ROLLBACK. You cannot “catch and continue” in the middle of a plain transaction. To recover from an expected failure without throwing away everything before it, use a savepoint — a named marker you can roll back to, undoing only the work after it:

sql

BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO orders (customer_id) VALUES (42);
  SAVEPOINT before_lines;
  INSERT INTO order_lines (order_id, sku) VALUES (1001, 'OUT-OF-STOCK');
  -- that raised an error; the txn is aborted *back to the savepoint*
  ROLLBACK TO SAVEPOINT before_lines;   -- keep the order, drop the bad line
  INSERT INTO order_lines (order_id, sku) VALUES (1001, 'IN-STOCK');
COMMIT;

Keep transactions short. This is the operational rule that matters most, and it comes straight from the mechanisms above. A transaction holds its locks and its MVCC snapshot from BEGIN until it ends — so a long-running or, worse, an idle-in-transaction one blocks other writers, and it pins old row versions so VACUUM can't reclaim them, letting table bloat grow. Do slow work (call an API, wait for user input) outside the transaction; open BEGIN, do the writes, COMMIT, and get out. That single discipline prevents a large share of production database incidents — and it's the bridge into Phase 5.

Step back and the four letters resolve into a simple mental model. Two of them are guarantees you basically get for free on every committed transaction: Atomicity (all or nothing) and Consistency (your declared constraints hold). The other two are dials — Isolation (how much concurrency safety, traded against throughput and retries) and Durability (how synchronously a commit is flushed, traded against latency). Knowing which are switches and which are dials is most of what senior database judgment consists of.

The posture to carry forward

When you design a write path, ask the four questions in order: Atomic? — is this one BEGIN…COMMIT so a partial result is impossible? Consistent? — is every invariant a declared constraint, not a hope? Isolated? — at what level, and which anomalies can still bite? Durable? — is the default synchronous_commit right for this data's value? Two of those answers are almost always yes; the other two are decisions. Phase 5 owns the third question and Phase 7 owns the fourth.
