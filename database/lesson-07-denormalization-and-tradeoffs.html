Denormalization & Trade-offs

Lesson 06 taught you to remove redundancy so every fact lives in exactly one place. This lesson does the opposite — on purpose. Denormalization is deliberately storing a fact more than once, or precomputing a value you could have derived, to make a read faster or simpler. It is a real tool. It is also a debt: the moment a fact lives twice, the two copies can drift apart and start returning different answers. The whole skill is knowing when the read is worth the risk — and only after you have normalized and measured.

00 The trade

Breaking the rule on purpose

Normalization gave you a discipline: one fact, one place. A customer's city lives in the customer row and nowhere else, so an UPDATE changes it once and everyone who joins to it sees the new value instantly. That design is correct by construction — there is no second copy to fall out of date. Denormalization walks back exactly that guarantee, and it does so for one reason: reads.

Here is the one-sentence definition to anchor the lesson: denormalization is the deliberate introduction of redundancy — a duplicated value, a precomputed aggregate, an embedded child — into an otherwise-normalized schema, to make a specific read faster or simpler, in exchange for more expensive writes and the risk that the copies disagree. Notice every word is a trade. You get something now (a cheaper read); you pay for it later, forever, on every write.

What you buy

  • A hot read gets faster — fewer joins, no re-aggregation at query time.
  • A query gets simpler — the value is just sitting in a column.
  • Sometimes the only way to hit a latency target at scale.

What you pay

  • Write cost — every write must now update the copy too.
  • Drift risk — miss one write path and the copies disagree.
  • More moving parts — a trigger, a job, or a queue to maintain and monitor.

Because the cost is permanent and the benefit is specific, the order of operations matters more than the technique itself. There is a rule, and it is not optional:

The rule of order

Normalize first. Measure. Denormalize only a read you have proven is hot. A clean, fully-normalized schema is your default and your source of truth. You reach for denormalization when a real, measured read is too slow and the usual fixes — an index, a better query — are not enough. Denormalizing a read you have not measured is just adding a bug (drift) to buy a speedup you cannot demonstrate.

Keep the framing sharp: this is a correctness-for-latency trade. Normalization is on the correctness side of the dial; denormalization spends correctness to buy latency. That is a legitimate thing to spend — databases do it internally all the time — but you should always know the exchange rate, and you should always be able to say out loud what you gave up.

01 The catalogue

The forms of denormalization

“Storing a fact twice” shows up in a handful of recognizable shapes. Learn to name them, because each buys a different kind of read and each is paid for differently. Three of them put a literal copy into your tables; the fourth keeps a whole query result warmed up off to the side.

Duplicated column

  • Copy a column from a parent onto the child, e.g. customer_name onto orders.
  • Speeds up: reads that no longer need the join.
  • Costs: a rename of the customer must reach every order row, or they drift.

Precomputed aggregate

  • Store a rollup you could compute, e.g. orders.total or users.post_count.
  • Speeds up: reads that skip a SUM/COUNT over many rows.
  • Costs: every insert/delete of a line item must adjust the stored number, exactly.

Collapsing / embedding

  • Fold a child table into the parent as a jsonb blob or an array.
  • Speeds up: reads that fetch the whole object in one row, no join.
  • Costs: you lose per-child constraints, and partial updates get awkward.

The first two are the everyday ones. A duplicated column is the purest form of redundancy: the same string now sits in two tables, and truth depends on both being kept identical. A precomputed aggregate (or counter) is subtler and more dangerous, because the copy is derived — it must equal the count of something else, so a single missed increment silently makes it wrong forever. We give the counter its own worked example in section 04, because it hides a classic concurrency bug.

sql

-- a precomputed counter, denormalized onto users
ALTER TABLE users
  ADD COLUMN post_count integer NOT NULL DEFAULT 0;
-- the true value is count(*) of that user's posts; now it lives in two places

Collapsing is a modeling decision as much as a performance one: instead of a separate order_items table you might store the items as a jsonb array on the order. That is genuinely faster to read as a unit, but you have traded away the relational guarantees Lesson 06 fought for — there is no foreign key on an item inside a blob, no easy UNIQUE, no cheap “all items priced above X across all orders.” Phase 6 covers jsonb in depth; for now, treat embedding as denormalization with an even bigger correctness price.

The fourth form: materialized views

A materialized view is a maintained copy of a whole query's result, stored like a table. You write the query once as a view, then REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW recomputes and stores its rows. It is denormalization at the query level: blazing-fast reads of a complex aggregate, paid for by staleness — the result reflects the world as of the last refresh, not right now. It is the natural tool for dashboards and reports, and it gets a full deep-dive in Phase 6. Mentally file it here: same trade, larger unit.

02 The core hazard

The drift problem ▸

Every form above shares one failure mode, and it is worth staring at until it feels inevitable. The instant a fact lives in two places, the two places can disagree — and once they disagree, a reader who hits the stale copy gets a wrong answer while everything looks fine. Nothing crashes. No error is raised. The data is simply, quietly incorrect.

Picture a normalized world first: the customer's city lives in the orders row, the single source of truth. Now suppose that, to print invoices faster, someone copied the city onto each invoice row at invoice time. Watch what one ordinary UPDATE does to that copy:

The dangerous step is the third one. Nothing is broken — the source is perfectly up to date, the copy is perfectly readable, and the two just happen to hold different strings. The bug only becomes visible when a reader picks the wrong one, and by then it looks like a random data glitch rather than what it is: a fact that was never allowed to live in two places without a mechanism to keep them equal. That mechanism is the entire subject of the next section.

Redundancy is a promise you now have to keep

A normalized schema keeps consistency for free — there is only one copy, so it cannot disagree with itself. Denormalization converts that free guarantee into a chore: you now owe an update to the copy on every write path that touches the source. Drift is simply what happens when you miss one of those paths. The question is never “will it drift?” — it is “what keeps it from drifting, and how stale can it get?”

03 The mechanisms

Keeping copies in sync ▸

If you are going to store a fact twice, you must choose how the copy is kept equal to the source — and that choice is really a choice about consistency vs. coupling. There are four common answers, and they land at different points on the spectrum from “always exactly right” to “right eventually.”

  • Application dual-write — your app code writes the source, then writes the copy. Simple to picture, but racy and fragile: the two writes are separate steps, so a crash or a concurrent path can leave the copy behind. The honest version of this pattern is the transactional outbox (Phase 8) — don't hand-roll two independent writes.
  • Database trigger — a trigger updates the copy inside the same transaction as the source change. Because they commit together, the copy is always consistent: a reader can never see the source move without the copy moving too. The cost is coupling and write overhead on every change.
  • Materialized view — recompute the copy in a batch with REFRESH. Dead simple and decoupled, but stale between refreshes: the copy is correct only as of the last refresh.
  • Change data capture (CDC) — an async consumer reads the WAL and updates the copy after commit. Scales well and keeps systems decoupled, but is eventually consistent: there is always a window where the copy lags.

The cleanest contrast is between the two extremes — the synchronous trigger and the asynchronous queue. One commits the copy atomically with the source; the other lets the source commit now and repairs the copy later. Follow a single write down both paths:

The trigger's superpower is the shared transaction: the source change and the copy change either both commit or both roll back, so no reader ever observes them out of step. You pay for that with write amplification and lock coupling — every insert now also touches (and locks) the counter row. The async path pays a different bill: the source write stays cheap and independent, but you accept a staleness window and the operational weight of a consumer that can lag, fail, or fall behind. Neither is free; they just charge you in different currencies.

Which mechanism, in one line each

Need the copy exactly right, always? Trigger (synchronous, in-transaction). Can tolerate minutes of staleness on a heavy aggregate? Materialized view (batch refresh). Crossing a system boundary at scale? CDC (async, eventually consistent). Tempted to just write both from app code? Don't — reach for the outbox pattern in Phase 8 instead of two naked writes.

04 Worked example

A worked counter cache ▸

Let's make the trade concrete with the most common denormalized value of all: a counter. Say users.post_count should always equal the number of that user's posts. It is a precomputed aggregate — a copy of count(*) — and keeping it correct turns out to be a perfect illustration of why the sync mechanism matters. The safe way is a trigger that bumps the counter in the same transaction as the post insert:

sql

CREATE FUNCTION bump_post_count() RETURNS trigger
LANGUAGE plpgsql AS $$
BEGIN
  UPDATE users
     SET post_count = post_count + 1   -- read-modify-write, atomic, inside the DB
   WHERE id = NEW.author_id;
  RETURN NEW;
END;
$$;

CREATE TRIGGER posts_count_ins
  AFTER INSERT ON posts
  FOR EACH ROW EXECUTE FUNCTION bump_post_count();
-- a matching AFTER DELETE trigger does post_count = post_count - 1

The reason this is correct is subtle and important: SET post_count = post_count + 1 is a single statement that reads and writes the row atomically, under a row lock, entirely inside the database. Two concurrent inserts take turns on that row — the second waits for the first to commit, then adds to the already-updated value. The counter cannot lose an increment.

Now contrast the tempting shortcut: maintain the counter in application code by reading it, adding one, and writing it back. This splits the atomic read-modify-write into two round-trips with a gap in the middle — and that gap is exactly the lost update you met in Lesson 00's balance race and Lesson 03's concurrency notes:

sql

-- BROKEN: read then write, split across a network round-trip
n := SELECT post_count FROM users WHERE id = 7;   -- both sessions read 5
UPDATE users SET post_count = 6 WHERE id = 7;      -- both write 6 → one +1 is lost

-- SAFE: one atomic statement; no read-modify-write in app code
UPDATE users SET post_count = post_count + 1 WHERE id = 7;

The lesson generalizes past counters. Any denormalized value that is maintained by reading the old copy and writing a new one is exposed to the lost-update race unless that read-and-write is a single atomic operation — a trigger in the same transaction, an in-database SET x = x + 1, or an explicit lock. Push that arithmetic into application code and you have reintroduced the very bug transactions exist to prevent. And remember the counter's other cost: a single hot row that every insert must lock can become a contention point, which is sometimes reason enough not to keep the counter at all.

Callback: the lost update never left

This is the same failure as the bank-balance race in Lesson 00 — two readers of a shared value, both writing back a result computed from a now-stale read. Denormalized counters are simply where it ambushes people who thought they were done thinking about concurrency. Keep the increment inside the database, in one statement, and the race disappears. Phase 5 gives you the full vocabulary (row locks, isolation levels) for why.

05 Reason about it

The decision rule

Let's turn all of this into a checklist you can run in your head — or better, in a design review — before you denormalize anything. Imagine the classic trigger: a product page is slow because rendering it re-counts every user's posts and re-sums every order. The instinct is to cache those numbers in columns. Before you do, walk the four gates. If any one fails, you are not ready.

  • (1) A measured read problem. Not “this feels slow” — an actual number: p95 latency, a query plan, a profile. You cannot justify a permanent write cost with a hypothetical read.
  • (2) A chosen sync mechanism. Decide now, explicitly, how the copy stays equal to the source — trigger, materialized view, CDC — and what staleness that implies. “We'll keep it updated” is not a mechanism.
  • (3) Monitoring for drift. Have a way to detect when the copy diverges — a periodic reconciliation query comparing the copy to count(*), an alert, a nightly check. Redundancy you don't audit will silently rot.
  • (4) It's written down. The redundancy, its source of truth, its sync path, and its staleness bound belong in a comment and a doc. The next engineer must not discover the copy by breaking it.

Here is the move most people skip: before any of that, try an index. A huge fraction of “we need to denormalize” problems are really “the planner is scanning a table it could be seeking.” An index (Phase 4) makes the read fast without introducing a second copy at all — no drift, no sync mechanism, no monitoring. It is strictly cheaper on the correctness axis. Denormalization is what you reach for when the read is still too slow after the schema is clean and the indexes are in place. In interview terms: “I'd first check the plan and add the right index; only if that's still not enough would I consider a maintained counter, with a trigger to keep it in the same transaction and a reconciliation job to catch drift” is the answer that shows judgment.

Where this shows up next

You've now seen the general shape of a problem you'll meet again at every scale. Phase 4 (indexing) is the fix to try before denormalizing. Phase 6 (materialized views & jsonb) is denormalization at the query and document level, with Postgres tooling to maintain it. And Phase 8 (scale & applied design) is the exact same “two copies, one truth” problem grown up — keeping a cache, a search index, or a read replica in sync with the database. Every one of them is a bet: read speed now against the correctness risk of a copy that can drift. Learn to price that bet, and you can make it deliberately instead of by accident.

★ Review

Summary & self-test

The whole lesson compresses to one idea: denormalization deliberately stores a fact twice to make a read faster, and the price is that the copies can drift — so you only do it for a measured hot read, with a chosen mechanism to keep the copies in sync, monitoring to catch drift, and it written down.
