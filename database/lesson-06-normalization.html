Normalization

Normalization has one plain goal hiding behind its formal names: every fact should live in exactly one place. When the same fact is copied across many rows, sooner or later two copies disagree — and now the database contradicts itself. This lesson builds the idea from the why (the anomalies redundancy causes) to the how (functional dependencies, and the ladder from 1NF to BCNF), all in concrete tables rather than academic symbols.

Original study notes. Written from scratch, PostgreSQL-based (targeting PG 17/18). Contains three interactive diagrams (press Play or step through with Next). This is a concept lesson — no SQL drill; it builds the mental model the rest of Phase 1 relies on. A word up front: normalization is about correctness, not speed — the deliberate trade against it (denormalization) is the very next lesson, L07.
00 The core idea

One fact, one place

Here is the whole subject in a sentence, and everything else is mechanism: store each independent fact exactly once, so there is no second copy that can drift out of sync. A customer's city is one fact. A product's price is one fact. If those facts get copied onto every order line the customer or product appears in, you now have dozens of copies of a single truth — and nothing stops one copy from being updated while the others are forgotten.

That is the danger, stated plainly: redundancy lets copies disagree. A well-designed schema doesn't rely on discipline ("remember to update all the rows"); it makes the disagreement impossible by never storing the second copy in the first place. Normalization is the systematic process of reshaping tables so that each fact has a single home.

It is worth being precise about what normalization is and isn't for, because this trips people up:

  • Normalization is about correctness — preventing a class of data-integrity bug that no amount of application code can fully guard against.
  • It is not primarily about saving disk space, though it usually does.
  • It is not about performance. In fact, a highly normalized schema often needs more joins to answer a question, which can be slower. Trading some normalization back for speed is a real, deliberate technique — but it is a separate decision, covered in L07. First get it correct.
The one-line test

Point at any piece of data in your schema and ask: "If this fact changed, how many rows would I have to update?" If the honest answer is "more than one," you have redundancy, and redundancy is a landmine. The goal of the next few sections is to make that answer always be one.

01 Why redundancy hurts

The three anomalies ▸

The cleanest way to feel why redundancy is dangerous is to build a table the naive way and watch it break. Imagine a single wide table that records every order line, and — for convenience — stashes the customer's name and city right there on each row:

the wide table

CREATE TABLE order_lines (
  order_id       bigint,
  product        text,
  customer_name  text,   -- copied onto every line
  customer_city  text,   -- copied onto every line
  qty            integer
);

Ana has three lines, so her city is written three times. That duplication is not just wasteful — it opens the door to three distinct kinds of failure, the classic update, insertion, and deletion anomalies. Step through each one:

Notice that all three anomalies share a single root cause — the customer's city is stored somewhere it doesn't belong, tangled up with order-line data. The update anomaly is a consistency risk (copies disagree). The insertion anomaly is an existence problem (you can't record a fact until an unrelated fact exists). The deletion anomaly is loss (removing one fact silently erases another). Fix the structure and all three disappear at once — you don't patch them individually.

Anomalies are structural, not accidental

It's tempting to say "we'll just be careful to update every row." But carefulness doesn't scale, and it can't survive concurrency, bugs, or a second engineer. The point of normalization is to make the anomaly unrepresentable — if the city is stored once, there is literally no second row to forget. Structure beats discipline.

02 The tool

Functional dependencies: the grammar of a fact

To reshape a table on purpose rather than by feel, you need one precise idea: the functional dependency. Written X → Y and read "X determines Y," it means: whenever two rows agree on X, they must agree on Y. Put concretely, customer_id → city says every row with the same customer has the same city — because a customer has exactly one city. A functional dependency is just a fact-shaped rule about your data.

The most important dependency in any table is the one on its key. A key determines the whole row: given the key value, every other column is fixed. That's the key's entire job — to be the single handle that pins down one fact-bearing row. Trouble starts when a column depends on something other than the whole key. Two shapes of trouble matter:

Full dependency ✓

  • (order_id, product) → qty
  • qty needs both parts of the composite key to be pinned down.
  • This is what you want: the column depends on the whole key.

Partial dependency ✗

  • product → price
  • Price depends on part of the composite key (product), not the whole thing.
  • The 2NF violation — the column is in the wrong table.

Transitive dependency ✗

  • order_id → customer → city
  • City depends on a non-key column (customer), which depends on the key.
  • The 3NF violation — a fact one hop removed.

Those two bad shapes — partial (depends on part of the key) and transitive (depends on a non-key column, which depends on the key) — are exactly the redundancies that caused the anomalies you just saw. Normalization is nothing more than hunting down these dependencies and giving each determinant its own table.

There's a famous mnemonic that compresses the entire target into one sentence. A table is well-formed when every non-key column depends on:

The creed

“The key, the whole key, and nothing but the key — so help me Codd.”
— the key (there must be a key at all: 1NF), the whole key (no partial dependencies: 2NF), and nothing but the key (no transitive dependencies: 3NF). Tighten "the key" to mean every determinant is a candidate key and you've stated BCNF in a single breath.

03 The ladder

1NF → 2NF → 3NF → BCNF ▸

The normal forms are a ladder: each rung removes one specific kind of bad dependency, and each assumes you've already climbed the rung below it. You don't need to memorize the formal definitions to use them well — you need to recognize what each one is removing. Here is the ladder in plain terms, applied to our wide order_lines table:

  • 1NF (atomic values). Every cell holds a single value — no comma-lists, no arrays-as-columns like product1, product2, product3, no repeating groups. One value per cell; one row per thing. Our wide table is already 1NF.
  • 2NF (no partial dependencies). Only an issue when the key is composite. Every non-key column must depend on the whole key, not just part of it. Product attributes depend on product alone — part of the key — so they move out.
  • 3NF (no transitive dependencies). No non-key column may depend on another non-key column. The city depends on the customer, and the customer on the order — a transitive hop — so customers move out too.
  • BCNF (every determinant is a candidate key). The strict form of 3NF: if column X determines anything, X must itself be a key. It closes an edge case 3NF can leave open. For most schemas, reaching 3NF already gives you BCNF for free.

Watch the wide table split, one rung at a time, until every fact has its own home:

Every split is a lossless decomposition: we can always reconstruct the original wide table by joining the pieces back on their keys, so no information is lost — only the redundancy is. That "join it back later" property is what makes the whole exercise safe. You are not throwing anything away; you are choosing to store each fact once and re-assemble the wide view on demand, which is exactly what the next section shows.

2NF only bites composite keys

If your table has a single-column key (a plain surrogate id), there is no "part of the key" to depend on, so 2NF is automatically satisfied and you can think in terms of 3NF directly. 2NF earns its own rung specifically for tables with composite keys — like our order_lines(order_id, product).

04 The result

The payoff: correctness for free ▸

Here is the schema the ladder produces. Four tables, each owning its own facts, wired together by foreign keys. Notice the deliberate choices: identity columns rather than SERIAL, a composite primary key on order_lines, numeric for money, timestamptz for time — and an explicit index on each foreign-key column, because Postgres does not create those automatically.

the normalized schema

CREATE TABLE customers (
  customer_id  bigint GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
  name         text NOT NULL,
  city         text NOT NULL
);

CREATE TABLE products (
  product_id   bigint GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
  name         text NOT NULL,
  price        numeric(10,2) NOT NULL          -- money: numeric, never float
);

CREATE TABLE orders (
  order_id     bigint GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
  customer_id  bigint NOT NULL REFERENCES customers,
  placed_at    timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

CREATE TABLE order_lines (
  order_id     bigint NOT NULL REFERENCES orders,
  product_id   bigint NOT NULL REFERENCES products,
  qty          integer NOT NULL CHECK (qty > 0),
  PRIMARY KEY (order_id, product_id)          -- the composite key
);

-- Postgres does NOT auto-index FK columns. Add them yourself:
CREATE INDEX ON orders (customer_id);
CREATE INDEX ON order_lines (product_id);
-- order_lines.order_id is already covered by the PK's leading column.

Now the two things the wide table did badly become easy. Ana changing city is a single-row update, and the old wide report is a JOIN computed on demand — never a stored duplicate. Step through both:

The query that rebuilds the original wide view is just the lossless decomposition, run backwards:

reassemble the wide report on demand

SELECT o.order_id, p.name AS product,
       c.name AS customer, c.city, ol.qty
FROM   order_lines ol
JOIN   orders    o ON o.order_id    = ol.order_id
JOIN   customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
JOIN   products  p ON p.product_id  = ol.product_id;

This is the deal normalization offers. You give up the convenience of one flat table and the ability to read a whole "row" without a join. In return, every fact is stored once, so it can never contradict itself; a change is a single small write; and the flat view is always available on demand, always current, because it's computed from the truth rather than copied from it. Correctness stops being something you maintain and becomes something the schema guarantees.

05 Reason about it

How far to go

So should you always push to the highest normal form you can? No — and knowing where to stop is the mark of someone who actually understands the trade, not just the rules. Let's reason through a real decision the way you would at a whiteboard.

Start with the default: aim for 3NF. It removes every anomaly we saw — update, insertion, deletion — and for the overwhelming majority of schemas it also happens to satisfy BCNF, so you get the strict guarantee for free. If you do nothing but reach 3NF on every table, you have a correct, professional schema. That is the target you carry into almost every design.

When do you reach past 3NF to BCNF? Only in the specific case 3NF permits and BCNF forbids: a table where some determinant is not a candidate key. This shows up with overlapping candidate keys — think a table where (student, subject) → teacher but also teacher → subject, so teacher determines a key column without being a key itself. It's uncommon, but when you spot a non-key determinant, that's your cue to decompose one step further. The higher forms — 4NF (multivalued dependencies) and 5NF (join dependencies) — solve genuinely rare problems; you should know they exist and reach for them only when a concrete anomaly forces you to. Most engineers go entire careers at 3NF/BCNF.

And when do you deliberately go the other direction, storing a fact twice on purpose? Only with a measured reason. The industry proverb captures the whole posture:

The working rule

“Normalize until it hurts, denormalize until it works.” Design normalized by default — it's the correct starting point. Then, only if a real, measured performance problem demands it, and only where you can afford to keep the copies in sync (or accept them going stale), trade a slice of normalization back for speed. That reverse move — and the machinery to keep the copies honest — is exactly what L07: Denormalization & when to break the rules is about.

Where this shows up next: the join you wrote to rebuild the wide report is the everyday cost of a normalized schema, and Phase 2 makes joins second nature. The deliberate reversal — and how to keep duplicated facts from drifting — is L07. And the very same "one fact, many copies" problem returns at system scale in Phase 8, where the copies live in different services or caches and there's no single table to enforce the truth — the hardest version of exactly what you learned to prevent here.

★ Review

Summary & self-test

The whole lesson compresses to one idea: store each fact in exactly one place, so no two copies can ever disagree — then reassemble any view you need with a join, on demand, rather than duplicating the fact. The normal forms are just a checklist for finding the copies and giving each its own home.
