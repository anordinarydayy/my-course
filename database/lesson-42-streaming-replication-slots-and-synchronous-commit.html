Streaming Replication, Slots & Synchronous Commit

A standby is not a backup and not magic — it is a second PostgreSQL that replays your WAL, continuously, and stays a copy of the primary just a little behind. This lesson opens the pipe: how the WAL byte-stream flows from primary to standby, why a replication slot both saves you and can fill your disk, and how the synchronous_commit dial trades commit latency for how much data you can afford to lose.

00 Orientation

A standby is a live copy of your WAL

Back in the durability lesson you met the write-ahead log (WAL): before PostgreSQL touches a data page, it first writes a record describing the change to a sequential log, and a COMMIT is durable once that log is flushed. Recovery after a crash is just replaying the WAL forward from the last checkpoint. Physical streaming replication takes that same WAL and, instead of only replaying it locally after a crash, ships it live to another server that replays it continuously. That other server is a standby.

Because it is replaying the exact byte-level WAL, a physical standby is a block-for-block copy of the primary — same tables, same indexes, same on-disk layout, always trailing by a small, measurable gap. It cannot diverge, and it cannot be written to directly; its only job is to keep replaying. That single mechanism buys you three very different things:

  • High availability — if the primary dies, a standby that is already caught up can be promoted to take over (that is failover, lesson 44).
  • Read scaling — a hot standby can serve read-only queries, spreading read load off the primary.
  • A near-real-time copy — for reporting, or to seed other systems.

Three knobs control the whole arrangement, and they are exactly this lesson's three diagrams: the stream (how WAL gets there), slots (how the primary knows what WAL to keep), and synchronous_commit (whether a commit waits for the standby before it returns to the client).

The one reframe to keep

Replication isn't a new subsystem bolted on — it is the durability machinery you already trust, pointed at a second machine. The WAL was always the source of truth for "what changed and in what order." Streaming replication just lets a second server subscribe to that stream. If you understand crash recovery, you already understand 80% of physical replication.

Physical, not logical — and neither is a backup

This lesson is physical replication: raw WAL bytes, whole-cluster, exact copy. There is also logical replication (row-level changes, selective, cross-version), the basis of CDC — that is lesson 45. And note the trap up front: a standby faithfully replays your mistakes. A DELETE without a WHERE reaches the standby in milliseconds. Replication is not a backup — you still need PITR.

01 The pipe

The WAL stream, end to end ▸

Follow a single committed change from the primary to the standby. Three named processes do the work: a walsender on the primary reads the WAL and streams it; a walreceiver on the standby receives it and writes it down; and the standby's startup process replays those records onto its data pages — the very same REDO code that runs during crash recovery, except here it never stops.

The number that measures "how far behind" is the LSN — the Log Sequence Number, a monotonically increasing byte offset into the WAL, written as 0/16C0000. The primary is always at some current LSN; each standby reports where it has gotten to. In fact it reports four positions, and knowing them is how you diagnose a lagging replica:

  • sent_lsn — the last WAL byte the walsender has sent.
  • write_lsn — the last byte the standby has written to its OS.
  • flush_lsn — the last byte the standby has fsync'd to disk (durable there).
  • replay_lsn — the last byte the standby has applied, and therefore what its read queries can see.
Two different lags hide in that list

Flush lag (has the standby persisted the WAL?) governs durability and failover data loss. Replay lag (has the standby applied it?) governs what a reader on the standby sees — and replay can fall behind flush, e.g. when a long query on the standby holds off conflicting replay. That gap between "received" and "visible" is exactly what makes read-after-write on a replica tricky, which is the whole of lesson 43.

02 Keeping the WAL

Replication slots — the safety net that can sink you ▸

Here's a problem the stream alone doesn't solve. The primary recycles old WAL segments on its own schedule to reclaim disk. If a standby is offline or slow and falls behind past the point where the WAL it still needs has already been recycled, the stream breaks — the standby asks for bytes that no longer exist and can only be rebuilt from scratch. A replication slot fixes that: it is a small piece of server-side state that records the standby's confirmed position (restart_lsn) and tells the primary, "do not recycle any WAL past this point until I've consumed it."

That guarantee is exactly what makes it dangerous. A slot keeps its promise even when the standby is gone. Watch both faces of it:

You create and inspect physical slots with a handful of functions. The two columns to watch on pg_replication_slots are active (is anyone consuming it?) and wal_status (reserved → extended → unreserved → lost) — a slot drifting toward lost is a live grenade:

sql

-- on the primary: create a physical replication slot
SELECT pg_create_physical_replication_slot('standby_a');

-- which slots exist, and how much WAL is each one pinning?
SELECT slot_name, active, wal_status, restart_lsn,
       pg_size_pretty(
         pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_current_wal_lsn(), restart_lsn)) AS retained
FROM   pg_replication_slots;

The defense is two settings and one habit. Since PostgreSQL 13 you can bound how much WAL any one slot is allowed to pin; past that limit the slot is invalidated (it goes lost) rather than taking the whole server down — you sacrifice the lagging standby to save the primary. And you drop slots the moment a standby is retired:

sql

-- cap how much WAL a single slot may pin (PG 13+)
ALTER SYSTEM SET max_slot_wal_keep_size = '10GB';
SELECT pg_reload_conf();

-- release a forgotten slot's WAL by dropping it
SELECT pg_drop_replication_slot('standby_a');
The classic 3 a.m. outage

Someone decommissions a replica, or a replica crashes over a long weekend, and nobody drops its slot. The primary dutifully retains every WAL segment since that slot's frozen LSN. Days later pg_wal fills the disk, the primary can no longer write WAL, and it stops accepting writes — a full outage caused by a replica that no longer exists. Alert on pg_replication_slots where active = false and on retained WAL size. Set max_slot_wal_keep_size. Drop dead slots. This exact incident shows up in postmortems constantly.

03 How much to wait

The synchronous_commit dial ▸

So far the stream is asynchronous: the primary commits, returns to the client immediately, and the WAL flows to the standby whenever it flows. Fast — but if the primary dies one millisecond after telling the client "committed," a transaction that never reached the standby is lost on failover. Whether you accept that is the synchronous_commit setting, and it is not a boolean — it is a five-position dial choosing how far along the durability path a commit waits before it returns to the client.

Two things gate whether the standby-facing positions do anything at all. First, the standby only counts as synchronous if it is named in synchronous_standby_names; otherwise even remote_apply behaves like local. Second — and this is the safety valve — you express a quorum, so a single standby's death doesn't stall you:

sql

-- require ANY 1 of two named standbys to confirm before COMMIT returns
ALTER SYSTEM SET synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (standby_a, standby_b)';
ALTER SYSTEM SET synchronous_commit = 'on';   -- wait for remote flush
SELECT pg_reload_conf();

-- one heavy report can opt out of the wait, just for itself
SET synchronous_commit = 'local';

That last line is the underused superpower: synchronous_commit is settable per transaction. You can run the cluster at on for the money-moving transactions and drop a bulk import or an analytics refresh to local (or off) where losing a few seconds of it on a crash is fine — buying throughput exactly where correctness allows it.

Asynchronous (off / local)

  • Commit returns without waiting for any standby.
  • Lowest latency, highest throughput.
  • Eventually consistent; a failover can lose the last few transactions (small, non-zero RPO).
  • The right default for most systems.

Synchronous (on / remote_apply)

  • Commit waits for a standby to confirm.
  • Zero data loss on failover (RPO = 0).
  • Every commit pays a network round-trip of latency.
  • Needs a quorum, or a lone standby's death blocks writes.
The default's fine print

synchronous_commit = on is the shipped default, but with no synchronous standby named it just means "flush locally" — identical to local. It only starts waiting for a standby once you populate synchronous_standby_names. So turning on synchronous replication is really about that second setting; the commit dial then decides how far the wait goes.

04 In practice

Standing it up, and watching the lag

The good news for PG 17/18 is that the defaults already suit replication — wal_level = replica and hot_standby = on are on out of the box. The primary needs enough sender and slot slots; then you clone the data directory with pg_basebackup and point the standby at the primary. On the primary side:

postgresql.conf (primary)

# these are already the PG 17/18 defaults — shown for clarity
wal_level             = replica   # minimal | replica | logical
max_wal_senders       = 10       # one per connected standby (+ base backups)
max_replication_slots = 10

On the standby, two lines say where the primary is and which slot to claim, and an empty standby.signal file in the data directory is what tells PostgreSQL to boot in standby mode and start streaming rather than open for writes:

postgresql.auto.conf (standby)

# how to reach the primary, and which slot to consume
primary_conninfo  = 'host=10.0.0.1 user=replicator application_name=standby_a'
primary_slot_name = 'standby_a'
# plus: an empty file named standby.signal in the data directory

Once it is streaming, one view is your dashboard. On the primary, pg_stat_replication has a row per connected standby — its state (should be streaming), its sync_state (async, potential, sync, or quorum), the four LSNs, and the three time-based lags PostgreSQL computes for you:

sql

-- on the PRIMARY: one row per connected standby
SELECT application_name, state, sync_state,
       sent_lsn, flush_lsn, replay_lsn,
       flush_lag, replay_lag
FROM   pg_stat_replication;

And on the standby itself, when you want to answer "how stale am I right now?" in human terms, ask how long ago the last replayed transaction was committed. When the standby is fully caught up on an idle primary this can read as a growing number — that is expected; it means "no new transactions," not "falling behind":

sql

-- on a STANDBY: how far behind is it, in time?
SELECT pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()                    AS replayed_lsn,
       now() - pg_last_xact_replay_timestamp() AS lag_time;
A preview: slots that survive failover (PG 17)

An ordinary replication slot lives only on the primary — promote a standby and the slot is gone, which is a real problem for CDC pipelines built on logical slots. PostgreSQL 17 added failover slots (failover = true, synchronized to standbys, plus pg_createsubscriber) so a slot can survive a primary failover. We keep that for lesson 45, where logical decoding and CDC take center stage — just know the capability exists.

05 Reason about it

Choosing the dial by your RPO

Put the three ideas together on a real decision. The lever you actually turn is synchronous_commit, and the right position is a business question, not a technical one: how much recently-committed data can this system afford to lose if the primary is destroyed? That tolerance is your RPO (Recovery Point Objective). Read it off the workload:

RPO ≈ 0

  • Payments, ledgers, "money must never vanish."
  • synchronous_commit = on (or remote_apply) over a quorum of standbys.
  • Pay the round-trip latency on every commit — knowingly.

RPO = seconds

  • Most apps: posts, orders, profiles.
  • Async (local) with a closely-watched replica.
  • Cheap and fast; a crash may lose the last moment of writes.

Mixed, per-txn

  • One cluster, two classes of work.
  • Cluster at on; drop bulk/analytics to local or off per transaction.
  • Correctness where it matters, speed where it doesn't.

Now walk the two failure modes this lesson warned about, because both are places engineers get surprised. The idle-slot disk-fill is a storage failure wearing a replication costume: a slot for a standby that no longer consumes keeps pinning WAL, and pg_wal grows until the primary can't write. The tell is a slot with active = false and a climbing retained size; the fix is monitoring, max_slot_wal_keep_size, and dropping dead slots. It is entirely preventable and entirely common.

The second is synchronous replication's availability trap. Set synchronous_standby_names to a single standby with synchronous_commit = on, and you have quietly coupled the primary's write availability to that one standby: if it dies, every commit blocks forever waiting for an acknowledgment that will never come. You went looking for durability and bought yourself an outage. The fix is a quorum over enough standbys (ANY 1 (a, b)) that one can fail without stalling writes — which is really just CAP arriving in a config line: under a partition you are choosing consistency (wait) over availability (proceed), so make sure enough replicas remain that "wait" still resolves.

The instinct to carry out of here

Before you enable synchronous replication, ask two questions: does the RPO justify a round-trip on every commit? and if my chosen sync standby dies, do I have enough others that writes keep flowing? If the answer to the first is "not really," stay async and spend your effort on monitoring lag instead. Durability you don't need is just latency you're paying for.

★ Review

Summary & self-test

The whole lesson compresses to one idea: a physical standby is your WAL, replayed continuously on a second server; a slot decides which WAL the primary must keep for it; and synchronous_commit decides how long a commit waits for that standby before it returns — trading latency for the data you can afford to lose.

02Three processeswalsender (primary) → walreceiver (standby) → startup process replays. Lag is the LSN distance between them.
03Four LSNssent / write / flush / replay. Flush lag governs failover data loss; replay lag governs what a reader on the standby sees.
04A slot pins WALIt makes the primary retain WAL until the standby confirms it, so a lagging standby can't fall irrecoverably behind.
05The idle-slot trapAn inactive, forgotten slot pins WAL forever → pg_wal fills the disk → the primary can't write. Monitor, cap, drop.
06A five-position dialoff → local → remote_write → on → remote_apply. Durability rises left to right; so does commit latency.
07Sync needs a quorumNaming one sync standby couples write availability to it; a quorum (ANY 1 of N) lets one fail without stalling commits.
08Choose by RPORPO 0 → sync over a quorum; seconds → async is cheaper. And replication is never a backup — it replays mistakes too.
