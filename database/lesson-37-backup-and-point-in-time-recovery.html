Backup & Point-in-Time Recovery

A backup you have never restored is a rumor, not a safety net. This practice lesson builds the one recovery technique that actually saves you when a human runs DELETE without a WHERE: Point-in-Time Recovery — one physical base backup plus every WAL segment since, replayed to a chosen second. You will separate logical from physical backups, watch PITR rewind a table to the instant before disaster, and write the full runbook — enable archiving, take the base backup, and recover to a timestamp — end to end.

37 Orientation

Two kinds of backup — and why one of them time-travels

Postgres gives you two fundamentally different ways to copy your data, and confusing them is the root of most backup disasters. The split is logical versus physical.

A logical backup captures the meaning of your data as SQL: CREATE TABLE and COPY/INSERT statements that rebuild the database when replayed. You take it with pg_dump (one database) or pg_dumpall (the whole cluster, including roles). It is portable across Postgres versions and even architectures, you can restore a single table, and it is human-readable — but it is a snapshot as of the moment the dump began, it is slow to produce and slower to restore on large data, and it can never land you on an arbitrary second.

bash · logical

# Logical: portable SQL, per database; restores into any compatible server
pg_dump    -Fc -d shop    -f shop.dump      # one database, custom (compressed) format
pg_dumpall --globals-only -f globals.sql    # cluster-wide roles & tablespaces

A physical backup copies the actual bytes on disk — the entire cluster's data directory, file by file. You take it with pg_basebackup. It is fast, it captures every database and index exactly as stored, and — this is the crucial part — it can serve as the base that WAL replay continues from. It is not portable across major versions, and it is all-or-nothing (the whole cluster, never a single table).

bash · physical

# Physical: byte-level copy of the whole cluster's data directory
pg_basebackup -D /backup/base -Fp -Xs -c fast -P

Neither of those alone can undo a mistake made after the backup ran — a nightly dump taken at 02:00 knows nothing about the bad DELETE at 14:03. The technique that can is Point-in-Time Recovery (PITR), and it is built from the physical side plus one more ingredient:

The one-line definition of PITR

PITR = one physical base backup + every WAL segment archived since it was taken. The base backup gives you the cluster as of some starting instant; the continuous stream of archived write-ahead log lets Postgres replay every committed change forward and stop at any second you name in between. That "stop at any second" is what turns a copy of yesterday into a machine that undoes this afternoon's accident.

37 The mechanism

How PITR is built: base + WAL ▸

Recall from the durability lesson that Postgres writes every change to the WAL (write-ahead log) before touching the data pages, in 16 MB segment files. PITR simply keeps those segments instead of recycling them. Two background activities run continuously: a base backup taken once (or on a schedule), and continuous archiving that copies each filled WAL segment somewhere safe — set by archive_command, or streamed live with pg_receivewal.

Restore, then, is two moves in the other direction: lay down the base backup as your starting point, then replay the archived WAL forward until you reach the target. Step through the whole loop — production on top, recovery on the bottom:

The mental model that survives the interview: the base backup is the floor, the WAL is the staircase, and the recovery target is the step you get off on. Take away the base and you have steps leading nowhere; take away the WAL and you are stuck on the floor at backup time; break the staircase — a single missing segment — and you can climb no higher than the gap.

Why this beats a nightly dump

A logical dump gives you a handful of discrete restore points — 02:00 today, 02:00 yesterday. PITR gives you a continuous range: any instant from when the base backup finished up to your most recently archived WAL. Your worst-case data loss shrinks from "up to 24 hours" to "up to one WAL segment," and you gain the ability to stop just before a specific bad event rather than rolling all the way back to last night.

37 The rescue

Rewind to one second before the mistake ▸

Here is the scenario PITR exists for. It is a normal afternoon; committed transactions are streaming into the WAL. At 14:03:07 someone runs DELETE FROM orders; — no WHERE, the whole table gone — and it commits. That DELETE is a perfectly valid, durably-logged change; ordinary crash recovery would faithfully redo it. PITR's trick is to stop replaying one second earlier, at 14:03:06, so the mistake is simply never applied. Watch the replay walk up the timeline and get off the staircase just in time:

Two subtleties are worth naming. First, the target is expressed as a commit time (recovery_target_time), and recovery stops before the first transaction whose commit is past the target — so pick a time strictly earlier than the mistake, not equal to it. Second, everything after the target is not deleted from the archive; it is simply not replayed onto this restored copy. If you later discover you wanted a slightly different instant, you restore the base again and pick a new target.

Time is not the only kind of target

recovery_target_time is the intuitive one, but you can also stop at a specific transaction id (recovery_target_xid), a WAL position (recovery_target_lsn), or a named marker you set in advance with SELECT pg_create_restore_point('before_migration') and then target with recovery_target_name. When you know the exact bad transaction, an xid or a named point is more precise than guessing a wall-clock second.

37 Discipline

Tools, retention, and the numbers that matter: RPO & RTO

You can wire up archive_command and pg_basebackup by hand — and you should do it once, to understand it — but in production almost nobody hand-rolls the plumbing. Three tools own this space, and each automates base backups, WAL archiving, retention, and (critically) the restore side:

  • pgBackRest — the most feature-complete: parallel, compressed, incremental and differential backups, encryption, and to-cloud storage. The common default for serious deployments.
  • Barman — a backup server that manages many Postgres hosts centrally, with catalogs and streaming or archived WAL.
  • WAL-G — lightweight, cloud-native, streams backups and WAL straight to S3/GCS; popular for Kubernetes and high-throughput setups.

Whatever the tool, three habits separate a backup that works from one that only looks like it does. Back up from a standby, not the primary, when you can — the heavy I/O of a base backup then lands on a replica instead of your production node. Get retention right: you must keep WAL at least as far back as your oldest base backup you still rely on, or that backup becomes unrestorable; and beware an orphaned replication slot silently pinning WAL forever. And the rule that trumps them all:

An untested restore is not a backup

A backup job that reports "success" every night proves only that you can write files. It says nothing about whether those files can be restored — and plenty of teams have discovered, mid-outage, that their backups were corrupt, incomplete, or missing a WAL segment. Schedule real restore drills: periodically rebuild the cluster from scratch into a scratch environment, run PITR to a random target, and confirm the data. The drill also measures your recovery time honestly.

That brings us to the two numbers every backup design is ultimately judged by. They are business decisions dressed as engineering settings:

RPO — Recovery Point Objective

  • How much data you can afford to lose, measured in time.
  • Set by your archiving lag: with WAL archived per 16 MB segment, worst case is one unshipped segment.
  • Shrink it with archive_timeout (force a segment switch on a timer) or by streaming with pg_receivewal — approaching near-zero loss.
  • "We can lose at most 5 minutes of orders."

RTO — Recovery Time Objective

  • How long you can afford to be down while restoring.
  • Set by base-backup size, restore bandwidth, and how much WAL must replay.
  • Shrink it with frequent base backups (less WAL to replay), fast storage, and rehearsed runbooks.
  • "We must be back within 30 minutes."

The two pull against cost in different directions, and against each other: more frequent base backups cut RTO (less replay) but cost I/O and storage; streaming WAL cuts RPO but adds a moving part. You do not pick "the best" — you pick the numbers the business will pay for, then engineer to hit them. And you prove you hit them with a drill, not a spreadsheet.

37 Worked runbook

Worked problem: recover the cluster to 14:03:06 ▸

Enough theory — here is the whole thing as a runbook you could execute under pressure. First the mental map of the six moves, then every command. The rust box is the ongoing archiving window; the garnet box is the moment you go live again:

1 · The starting state

Recovery is only possible if you prepared before the disaster. These two things must already have been running: continuous archiving turned on, and at least one base backup on hand. This is the config in postgresql.conf on the live primary (changing wal_level requires a restart):

conf · postgresql.conf on the primary

wal_level       = replica        # WAL detailed enough to rebuild pages
archive_mode    = on
archive_command = 'test ! -f /archive/%f && cp %p /archive/%f'
# %p = path to the segment to archive, %f = its filename
# the test refuses to overwrite an existing archived segment

And the base backup itself, taken (ideally from a standby) at some earlier time:

bash

pg_basebackup -D /backup/2026-07-04 -Fp -Xs -c fast -P
# -Fp plain files, -Xs stream WAL during the copy, -c fast checkpoint, -P progress

2 · The task

At 14:03:07 a bad DELETE FROM orders; committed and wiped the table. You have the archive and the morning's base backup. Bring a cluster back holding the data exactly as of 14:03:06, one second before the mistake.

3 · The naive attempt, and why it is wrong

The tempting shortcut is to reach for last night's logical dump:

bash · wrong

# WRONG: restore the nightly logical dump
pg_restore -d shop /backup/shop-0200.dump

This fails on two counts. It restores the state at 02:00, silently discarding twelve hours of orders that had nothing to do with the accident — a catastrophic RPO. And a dump has no notion of "a second"; there is simply no knob to stop it at 14:03:06. A logical dump is a fine portable export and a fine coarse fallback, but it is not point-in-time, and using it here trades one data-loss event for a bigger one.

4 · The fix: PITR to a timestamp

Restore the physical base, then let WAL replay carry it forward to the target. On a fresh host (never overwrite the damaged primary until you have investigated), the recovery moves:

bash

# a. stop any server on this host; b. clear the target data dir
pg_ctl -D /var/lib/pgsql/data stop
rm -rf /var/lib/pgsql/data/*

# c. unpack the base backup into a fresh $PGDATA
cp -a /backup/2026-07-04/* /var/lib/pgsql/data/

Now tell Postgres how to fetch archived WAL and where to stop, in the restored cluster's config:

conf · postgresql.conf on the restored copy

restore_command        = 'cp /archive/%f %p'   # how to pull an archived segment
recovery_target_time   = '2026-07-04 14:03:06+00'
recovery_target_action = 'promote'              # open read/write once the target is reached

Finally, the signal file is what tells Postgres to enter archive recovery at startup rather than normal crash recovery — its mere presence is the switch (this replaced the old recovery.conf in PG 12):

bash

touch  /var/lib/pgsql/data/recovery.signal
pg_ctl -D /var/lib/pgsql/data start

5 · Why it is right — read the recovery log

Postgres now pulls segments via restore_command, replays each transaction, and watches the commit timestamps. The log tells the whole story — note the line where it stops before the 14:03:07 commit:

text · illustrative recovery log

LOG:  starting point-in-time recovery to 2026-07-04 14:03:06+00
LOG:  restored log file "000000010000000000000006" from archive
LOG:  restored log file "000000010000000000000007" from archive
LOG:  recovery stopping before commit of transaction 5142, time 2026-07-04 14:03:07.421+00
LOG:  redo done at 0/7A3C120
LOG:  selected new timeline ID: 2
LOG:  archive recovery complete
LOG:  database system is ready to accept connections

Two lines are the payoff. "recovery stopping before commit of transaction 5142" is PITR refusing to replay the DELETE — the whole point. "selected new timeline ID: 2" means the cluster forked a fresh timeline at the target: everything from here diverges from the old history, so a later restore can tell your recovered branch apart from the original. SELECT count(*) FROM orders; now returns the pre-disaster rows.

6 · Narrate it

In an interview, say it in five sentences: "Archiving was already on — wal_level=replica, archive_mode=on, and an archive_command shipping every WAL segment — and I had a recent pg_basebackup. To recover, I restored that base into a fresh data directory, set restore_command so Postgres could fetch archived WAL, and set recovery_target_time to one second before the bad DELETE. I dropped a recovery.signal file and started the server; it replayed WAL forward and stopped before the destructive commit, exactly as the log's 'recovery stopping before commit' line shows. Then it promoted onto a new timeline, and the table was whole." That is a complete, correct PITR — and it works precisely because the archiving discipline existed before anything went wrong.

37 Drills

Graded drills & traps

Attempt each on paper before opening its answer. They all assume the archiving-and-base-backup setup from the runbook is in place.

Drill 1 — Recover as far forward as possible

Different disaster: the storage under your primary died — no bad transaction to dodge, you just want the least data loss possible. Configure recovery to replay all archived WAL to the very end.

Try it first, then read

Set no recovery_target_* at all. With recovery.signal present and a restore_command, recovery replays every segment it can fetch and stops only when the archive runs out — your RPO is then one WAL segment:

restore_command = 'cp /archive/%f %p'
# no recovery_target_time / _xid / _lsn — replay to the end of the archive

Why: a recovery target only ever tells Postgres where to stop early; omit it and "as far as the WAL goes" is the default.

Drill 2 — Recover to a marker you set before a risky change

You are about to run a scary schema migration. Plant a labeled restore point first, so that if the migration goes wrong you can rewind to exactly that spot without guessing a timestamp.

Try it first, then read

Create the named point on the live primary before the migration, then target it by name during recovery:

-- on the live primary, right before the migration:
SELECT pg_create_restore_point('before_v42_migration');

# then, if you must recover, in postgresql.conf:
recovery_target_name = 'before_v42_migration'

Why: a named point is exact and self-documenting — no wall-clock guessing about when the migration started.

Drill 3 — Stop just before a specific bad transaction

From the recovery log you know the offending change was transaction id 5142. Recover to the state immediately before 5142 committed — more precise than a one-second-earlier timestamp.

Try it first, then read

Target the xid and turn off inclusivity so the target transaction itself is not applied:

recovery_target_xid       = '5142'
recovery_target_inclusive = off   # stop BEFORE 5142, not after

Why: recovery_target_lsn works the same way for a WAL position; inclusive = off is what excludes the poisoned transaction rather than replaying it.

Drill 4 — Recover onto a branch you already forked

You did a PITR last week, promoting onto timeline 2, and the cluster has run there since. Today you need to PITR again to a point on timeline 2's history. What does recovery_target_timeline control, what is its default, and when would you set it explicitly?

Try it first, then read

Tell recovery to follow the newest branch at every fork:

recovery_target_time     = '2026-06-28 09:15:00+00'
recovery_target_timeline = 'latest'   # the default since PG 12 — follow forks automatically

Why: every promotion mints a new timeline. Since PostgreSQL 12 recovery_target_timeline defaults to 'latest', so a fresh restore already follows the newest fork (timeline 2) — you get your branch for free. You set it explicitly (to a specific id, or 'current') only when you deliberately want to stop on an earlier line. On pre-12 the default was 'current' and you had to set 'latest' by hand — the classic gotcha this drill guards against.

The five traps that turn a "backup" into an outage

(1) A gap in the WAL archive. Recovery replays segments in sequence; a single missing or corrupt segment stops it dead at the gap — you can recover no further. Monitor archiving and use an archive_command that fails loudly so a broken copy blocks WAL recycling instead of being lost.
(2) Treating pg_dump as point-in-time. A dump is a snapshot as of its start and restores slowly; it cannot stop at an arbitrary second. It is a portable export, not a PITR mechanism.
(3) Never running a restore drill. "Backup succeeded" only proves you wrote files. Untested, corrupt, or incomplete backups are discovered mid-incident. Rehearse the restore and time it.
(4) Replaying everything after a mistake. Plain recovery redoes the bad DELETE too. You must set a recovery_target (and usually inclusive = off) to stop before it.
(5) Confusing replication with backup. A standby faithfully replays the DELETE within seconds — replication protects against a dead node, not a bad command. You still need PITR.

Summary & self-test
