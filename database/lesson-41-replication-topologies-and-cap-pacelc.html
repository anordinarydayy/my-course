Replication Topologies & the CAP/PACELC Map

One machine is two problems at once: a single point of failure and a read ceiling. Replication answers both by keeping live copies of your data on other machines — but the moment you have copies, you inherit a law of distributed systems. This lesson draws the topologies (one primary, many standbys) and then hands you the two frames — CAP and its sharper cousin PACELC — that every choice in the rest of this phase is really a point on.

Original study notes. Written from scratch, using PostgreSQL (PG 17/18) as the concrete system — physical streaming replication, hot standbys, and the synchronous_commit dial. 00 Why copies

The single-server ceiling

Everything you have learned so far assumes one PostgreSQL server holding one copy of the data. That server is doing an extraordinary job — ACID, MVCC, the planner — but it has two hard limits that no amount of tuning removes. First, it is a single point of failure: if its disk dies, its process crashes and can't restart, or its rack loses power, your application is down, and (without a recent, restorable backup) some data may be gone for good. Second, it is a read ceiling: one box has a finite number of CPUs and a finite amount of RAM for the buffer cache, so there is a hard cap on how many queries per second it can serve.

Replication attacks both. The idea is simple: keep one or more additional servers, each holding a continuously-updated copy of the data, so that a copy can take over if the original fails (availability), and so that read traffic can be spread across the copies (scale). Every distributed-database feature in this phase is built on that one move — making copies — so it pays to fix the vocabulary now.

  • Primary (also leader, historically master) — the one server that accepts writes. In classic Postgres replication there is at most one primary at a time.
  • Standby / replica (also follower) — a server that continuously receives the primary's changes and replays them to stay a near-exact copy. A hot standby is a standby that can also serve read-only queries while it replays.
  • Synchronous vs asynchronous — the single most consequential setting: does the primary's COMMIT wait for a standby to confirm the change (synchronous), or return immediately and let the change reach the standby a beat later (asynchronous)? That one question is the whole back half of this lesson.

Concretely, a standby connects to the primary and streams its write-ahead log; the primary can tell you who is connected and how far behind each one is:

sql

-- run on the PRIMARY: who is replicating, and how far behind?
SELECT application_name, state, sync_state, replay_lag
FROM   pg_stat_replication;

Here sync_state is one of async, potential, sync, or quorum, and replay_lag is how long ago the data the standby just applied was committed on the primary. Keep that replay_lag column in mind — it is the physical reality behind everything CAP and PACELC will describe abstractly.

The trade you just signed up for

The instant you go from one copy to two, you stop having a single source of truth that everyone sees at the same moment. There is now a primary's view and a standby's view, and — because the standby is a beat behind — they can disagree. Managing that disagreement, under normal operation and under failure, is the entire subject of distributed data. CAP and PACELC are just the names for the rules of that game.

01 The shapes

Topologies & routing ▸

The default and most important topology in PostgreSQL is one primary streaming to one or more standbys. The mechanism is physical streaming replication: the primary ships its raw WAL byte-stream to each connected standby, and each standby replays those WAL records to keep its data files a near-identical copy. Because the standbys share the exact same on-disk format, this is fast and faithful — but it also means the whole cluster runs the same major Postgres version and copies the whole cluster, not selected tables (that's logical replication, Lesson 42).

A single primary can fan out to many standbys, and a standby can itself feed a further standby — cascading replication — which offloads the streaming work from a busy primary. Step through how the shape grows, and where writes and reads are allowed to go:

Two routing rules fall straight out of the picture. Writes must go to the primary — a standby is read-only, and attempting an INSERT/UPDATE there raises "cannot execute … in a read-only transaction." Reads may go to standbys, which is how replication buys read scale-out: add replicas, point read traffic at them, and the primary is freed to handle writes. The application (or a proxy such as PgBouncer/HAProxy, or a pooler) is responsible for that split. Setting up a standby is mostly a matter of telling it where the primary is:

postgresql.conf · on the standby

# stream from the primary, over a dedicated replication slot
primary_conninfo  = 'host=10.0.0.1 user=replicator application_name=standby1'
primary_slot_name = 'standby1'
hot_standby       = on      # allow read-only queries while replaying WAL
At most one primary

The rule that keeps this whole design sane is exactly one writable primary at a time. Every standby derives its state from that one primary's WAL, so there is a single, ordered history of changes. If two servers ever accept writes at once (split-brain, Lesson 44), that single history forks and the copies diverge — the worst failure in the book. The topology's simplicity is precisely what prevents it.

02 The first law

CAP: what you must give up under a partition ▸

Now the copies have a cost, and CAP is its name. The CAP theorem concerns three properties of a distributed data store: Consistency (every read sees the latest write — one agreed-upon value), Availability (every request gets a non-error answer), and Partition-tolerance (the system keeps working even when the network drops messages between nodes). The theorem's real content is narrow and sharp: when a network partition happens, you cannot have both C and A — you must sacrifice one.

The word "partition" is not optional here: on a real network, links do fail, so P is not something you get to opt out of. The genuine choice is what to do during a partition — refuse to answer (keep C, lose A) or answer with possibly-stale data (keep A, lose C). Where does classic Postgres primary/standby sit? On the write path it leans CP: there is one primary, and a standby cut off from it simply cannot accept writes (it stays read-only), so during a partition the minority side becomes unavailable rather than accepting conflicting writes and diverging. But the moment you let a standby serve reads, those reads are an AP choice you opted into: the disconnected standby will happily answer, just with stale data. The lesson is that CAP is not a single label you stamp on your whole database — it is a decision you make per operation and per path.

CAP's fine print, often misquoted

"Pick two of three" is the popular slogan and it is misleading. You do not get to drop P — networks partition whether you like it or not. CAP only bites during a partition, and then it is a choice between C and A. That narrowness is exactly why PACELC exists: it asks the more useful question about the other 99.9% of the time, when there is no partition.

03 The fuller law

PACELC: the dial behind the whole phase ▸

CAP tells you nothing about ordinary operation, when the network is fine. Yet you still make a trade every single day — between how fast a write confirms and how current your replicas are. PACELC (Daniel Abadi, 2012) captures both halves in one line: if there is a Partition, choose Consistency or Availability; Else (normally), choose Latency or Consistency. The "Else" branch is the one you live in almost all the time, and it is exactly the synchronous-vs-asynchronous decision.

Follow a single COMMIT and ask the PACELC question — does the primary wait for the standby, or not?

In Postgres, that dial has a real name: synchronous_commit, with a graded spectrum from fast-and-loose to slow-and-safe — off → local → remote_write → on → remote_apply. Toward off/local you optimize Latency (commits don't wait for a replica) at the cost of a stale-read window and some data-loss risk on a crash; toward remote_apply you optimize Consistency (a committed write is already visible on the standby) at the cost of a network round-trip on every commit. Nothing about replication is free; PACELC is just the honest accounting of which currency you're spending.

The one frame to carry through Phase 8–9

Every knob ahead is a point on the PACELC map. Synchronous replication? You bought Consistency with Latency. Reading from a lagging replica? You bought Latency/Availability with Consistency. Quorum commit? You're tuning where on the C–L axis you sit. When a later lesson asks "which should I use," translate it to "which letter am I willing to give up, here, for this operation?" — and the answer usually writes itself.

04 The everyday trade

Synchronous vs asynchronous — and three myths

Zoom in on the "Else" branch, because it is where you actually operate. The choice between synchronous and asynchronous replication is usually framed with two operational numbers: RPO (Recovery Point Objective — how much recently-committed data you can afford to lose) and RTO (Recovery Time Objective — how long you can afford to be down). Async and sync land in very different places:

Asynchronous (the default)

  • COMMIT returns as soon as the primary's WAL is durable locally.
  • Fast writes; the primary never waits on a replica.
  • Standbys are eventually consistent — a small lag window.
  • RPO > 0: a primary crash can lose the last few not-yet-shipped commits.
  • A slow or dead standby cannot stall the primary.

Synchronous

  • COMMIT waits for a standby to confirm (remote_apply ⇒ already visible there).
  • Zero-data-loss on the primary's failure — RPO = 0.
  • Reads on the confirmed standby are never stale.
  • Higher latency: a network round-trip per commit.
  • Availability risk: if the sole sync standby is down, commits block.

That last availability risk is the classic sync-replication footgun — one dead replica freezing all writes — and the standard cure is quorum commit: require an acknowledgement from any N of M standbys, so any one can be down without stalling the primary. Postgres expresses this with synchronous_standby_names:

sql · on the primary

-- wait for ANY 1 of two standbys to confirm — one can be down, writes still flow
ALTER SYSTEM SET synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (standby1, standby2)';
ALTER SYSTEM SET synchronous_commit        = 'remote_apply';
SELECT pg_reload_conf();

With the mechanics in hand, three myths cause more outages than any subtlety, so name them out loud:

  • Myth 1 — "a replica is a backup." It is not. Replication faithfully copies everything, including your mistakes: an accidental DELETE or a bad migration reaches every standby in milliseconds. A backup lets you go back in time (point-in-time recovery, Lesson 37); a replica only tells you where you are now. You need both.
  • Myth 2 — "reads from a replica are consistent." With async replication they are eventually consistent: right after a write, the same user reading a lagging standby can see their own change vanish (the read-your-own-write problem, Lesson 43). Stale reads are a feature of the design, not a bug to be surprised by.
  • Myth 3 — "synchronous replication is free zero-data-loss." It costs a round-trip on every commit, and, done naively (one required sync standby), it converts a replica outage into a primary outage. Zero-RPO is real, but you pay for it in latency and in careful quorum configuration.
Read scale-out is real, but bounded

Adding hot standbys genuinely multiplies read throughput — this is the workhorse reason many teams replicate. But it scales reads, not writes (every write still funnels through the one primary), and it hands your application the job of tolerating lag on the read path. That tension — more read capacity, more staleness — is exactly the ground Lesson 43 walks.

05 Reason about it

Choosing a topology by requirement

You now have the whole toolkit as a decision, not a recipe. Don't ask "what topology should I run?" — ask "which of three needs am I actually buying, and what does CAP/PACELC say it costs?" There are three classic drivers, and they are not the same:

Need: High Availability

  • Survive a primary failing with minimal downtime.
  • A standby + a cluster manager to promote it (Lesson 44).
  • Often sync/quorum so the promoted node loses nothing.

Need: Read scale

  • Serve more read QPS than one box can.
  • Several hot standbys + routing reads to them.
  • Usually async; you accept bounded lag (Lesson 43).

Need: Disaster recovery

  • Survive a whole region/site going down.
  • An async standby in another region + PITR backups.
  • Async by necessity (cross-region latency); RPO > 0.

Now walk three real requirements through the frame. "We need to serve 5× the read traffic and can tolerate data that is a second stale" → that is a PACELC Latency choice: async hot standbys, route reads to them, done. "We must never lose a confirmed order, even if the primary dies mid-second" → that is a PACELC Consistency (RPO = 0) choice: synchronous commit with a quorum so one standby outage can't freeze checkout. "We have to keep running if an entire data-centre burns" → that is a CAP Availability/DR posture: a cross-region async standby you can promote, backed by point-in-time backups, accepting that a hard failover may lose the last unshipped commits. Same building blocks, three different points on the map — chosen by which letter you were willing to spend.

Crucially, notice these needs can conflict: cross-region DR wants async (latency forbids sync across a continent), while zero-RPO wants sync. Real clusters resolve this by mixing — a synchronous local standby for HA/RPO and an asynchronous remote standby for DR — which is only legible once you see each replica as its own PACELC decision.

★ Review

Summary & self-test

The whole lesson compresses to one idea: replication makes copies to beat the single-server ceiling — one writable primary streaming to many read-only standbys — and the price of copies is a distributed-systems law: under a partition you trade Consistency for Availability (CAP), and even without one you trade Latency for Consistency (PACELC). Every later lesson in this phase is a point on that map.
