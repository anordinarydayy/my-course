Durability & the WAL

Every other guarantee a database makes is worthless if it forgets a commit. Durability is the promise that once COMMIT returns, your change survives a crash, a kill, or a power cut one millisecond later — and Postgres keeps it without making every commit crawl. The trick is one mechanism: the write-ahead log. This lesson shows exactly how a committed write is made permanent, why checkpoints exist, which dials trade durability for latency, and how recovery replays the log after a crash.

Original study notes. Written from scratch for engineers who want database depth that pays off in interviews and in real systems. The examples use PostgreSQL (PG 17/18), but write-ahead logging, checkpoints, and crash recovery are universal to serious storage engines. 00 Orientation

How a COMMIT survives a crash

Start with the naive design and watch it fail. Suppose, to honour durability, the database wrote the actual changed data pages to disk on every COMMIT. That would be safe — but ruinously slow, for two reasons. First, the rows you touch live in pages scattered all over the data files, so committing means a flurry of random writes, the single slowest thing a disk does. Second, an 8 KB page write is not atomic at the hardware level: a crash halfway through can leave a torn page, half old and half new, which is corruption, not durability.

The write-ahead log (WAL) sidesteps both. Instead of forcing the sprawling data pages to disk at commit time, Postgres writes a compact, append-only record describing the change — enough information to redo it — and forces only that to disk. The rule that gives the log its name: the log record for a change reaches durable storage before the corresponding data page does. That is write-ahead logging. The data pages themselves catch up lazily, in bulk, at a checkpoint.

Why a log beats the pages

Appending to the WAL is a sequential write — the one access pattern disks and SSDs are fastest at. Writing the data pages means random writes smeared across the files. The WAL turns "fsync on every commit" from expensive random I/O into cheap sequential I/O, and defers the random writes to a checkpoint where they can be batched. Same durability, a fraction of the cost.

Hold three facts from this section: durability is bought by the WAL, not by writing data pages eagerly; the log is written ahead of the data; and the win comes from turning random writes into a sequential append. Everything below is detail on those three.

01 The write path

What one committed UPDATE actually does ▸

Let's trace a single statement all the way to durable. When your backend process runs an UPDATE, three things live in RAM and one thing lives on disk, and the order they happen in is the whole point. The changed row sits in a page in shared_buffers (Postgres's page cache); a description of the change sits in the WAL buffer; and only at COMMIT is anything forced to disk — and what's forced is the WAL, not the data page.

Two subtleties reward a second look. First, the WAL record is small: it does not copy the whole page (usually), only the change and where it applies, tagged with an LSN (log sequence number) — a monotonically increasing address into the log. Every data page also stores the LSN of the last WAL record that modified it, which is precisely how recovery and replication know whether a page is up to date. Second, the only synchronous, latency-adding step is the fsync of the WAL at commit. The dirty data page never blocks your commit; it drifts out to disk later. That single deferral is why Postgres can commit tens of thousands of transactions per second on ordinary hardware.

The WAL is the source of truth on a crash

After a crash, the data files may be stale — they only hold what the last checkpoint flushed. The WAL holds everything committed since. That's the mental inversion to keep: the durable record of your data is the log, and the data files are a lazily-updated projection of it. Recovery rebuilds the files from the log.

02 Checkpoints

Checkpoints: paying down the dirty pages ▸

If the WAL kept growing and the dirty pages never went to disk, two things would break. The WAL would fill the disk forever, because you can never delete a log record whose change hasn't reached the data files. And recovery would have to replay the log from the beginning of time, taking longer the longer the server has run. The checkpoint fixes both at once: periodically, the checkpointer process flushes all currently-dirty pages to the data files, then writes a checkpoint record and a redo pointer marking how far the log has been made permanent.

Checkpoints are triggered by whichever of two limits arrives first. checkpoint_timeout (default 5 minutes) caps how much time passes between checkpoints; max_wal_size (default 1 GB) caps how much WAL volume accumulates. A write-heavy server will hit max_wal_size long before the timeout — and if you see very frequent, size-triggered checkpoints, that's usually the signal to raise max_wal_size. The flush itself is a spike of random I/O; checkpoint_completion_target (default 0.9) tells the checkpointer to smear those writes over ~90% of the interval instead of dumping them all at once, so the spike doesn't stall foreground queries.

The checkpoint trade-off in one line

Frequent checkpoints = shorter crash recovery (less WAL to replay) but more I/O and, because full_page_writes re-images each page on its first change after a checkpoint, more WAL volume. Infrequent checkpoints = less steady-state I/O but a longer recovery after a crash. Tuning checkpoints is choosing where on that line you want to sit. Watch it with pg_stat_checkpointer (PG 17+): a high num_requested relative to num_timed means size-triggered churn.

03 The dials

The durability dials you can actually turn

Durability is not a single on/off switch — it's a set of knobs, each trading a little safety for a little speed (or vice-versa). Three matter most, plus one optimization that happens automatically. Here they are in the config file, then explained:

postgresql.conf

wal_level = replica                    # minimal | replica (default) | logical
full_page_writes = on              # torn-page protection — leave it on
synchronous_commit = on            # off | local | remote_write | on | remote_apply
checkpoint_timeout = 5min          # time-based checkpoint trigger
max_wal_size = 1GB                  # size-based checkpoint trigger
checkpoint_completion_target = 0.9  # spread the flush to smooth the I/O spike

full_page_writes

  • Protects against torn pages: a crash mid-write leaving a page half-old, half-new.
  • On the first change to a page after each checkpoint, the whole page image is written to WAL, so recovery can restore it wholesale.
  • Cost: a WAL volume spike right after every checkpoint. wal_compression shrinks it. Keep it on.

synchronous_commit

  • The pure durability/latency dial: how long COMMIT waits before it returns.
  • Turning it off makes commits fast but risks losing the last fraction of a second on a crash.
  • It never corrupts — it only changes how many recent commits a crash may drop.

wal_level

  • replica (default): enough WAL to run standbys and physical backups.
  • logical: adds row-level info for logical replication / CDC.
  • minimal: least WAL, but blocks replication and PITR — rarely worth it.

The synchronous_commit ladder is worth memorizing, because it is exactly the tunable boundary between "fast" and "can't lose a byte." From least to most durable (and least to most latency):

  • off — don't wait for the WAL flush at all. The commit returns immediately; a background process fsyncs the WAL shortly after. A crash can lose the last few hundred milliseconds of commits, but the database stays consistent. Fastest.
  • local — wait until the WAL is fsynced to the local disk before returning. Locally durable; doesn't wait for any replica.
  • remote_write — also wait until a synchronous standby has received the WAL into its OS (not necessarily fsynced).
  • on (default) — wait for the local flush and, if synchronous standbys are configured, for them to flush too.
  • remote_apply — wait until a synchronous standby has replayed the change, so a read on that standby will see it. Strongest guarantee, highest latency.
Group commit — the free optimization

fsync is expensive, so Postgres amortizes it. When many transactions commit at nearly the same moment, their WAL flushes are batched into a single fsync that durably persists all of them at once — group commit. This is why commit throughput scales with concurrency instead of collapsing: a hundred concurrent commits can cost roughly one disk sync, not a hundred. commit_delay/commit_siblings can nudge the batching, but the default behavior already does the heavy lifting.

sql

-- how often checkpoints happen and how much they wrote  (PG 17+)
SELECT num_timed, num_requested, buffers_written
FROM pg_stat_checkpointer;

-- the live WAL position, and the redo point of the last checkpoint
SELECT pg_current_wal_lsn();
SELECT redo_lsn, checkpoint_lsn FROM pg_control_checkpoint();

-- force a checkpoint right now (superuser, or a role granted pg_checkpoint since PG15)
CHECKPOINT;
04 Recovery

Crash recovery: replaying the log ▸

Now the payoff. The power cuts. When Postgres restarts, it does not trust the data files — it knows they may be stale — so it enters recovery. It reads pg_control to find the last checkpoint's redo pointer, then replays the WAL forward from there, re-applying every logged change to the pages in order (the REDO pass). Transactions that reached a COMMIT record are restored; transactions that didn't are simply never made visible. Watch it play out:

One detail sets Postgres apart from textbook recovery and is worth stating precisely. Postgres recovery is REDO-only — there is no separate undo pass. It replays all WAL forward, which may momentarily re-apply changes from the uncommitted transaction B too. But because Postgres uses MVCC, every row version carries the transaction id that created it; a transaction with no commit record is treated as aborted, so B's row versions are simply never visible and are later reclaimed by VACUUM. Consistency falls out of MVCC, not out of physically unwinding the changes.

Standbys do the same thing, forever

A replica is essentially a server stuck in permanent recovery: it streams the primary's WAL and replays it continuously. The checkpoint's cousin on a replica is a restartpoint — the same idea, bounding how much WAL a standby must re-read if it restarts. That the recovery machinery and the replication machinery are the same WAL replay is the bridge to Phase 8.

05 Why it matters

Reason about it: the dial, and what the WAL feeds

Put yourself on call. A service is bottlenecked on commit latency, and profiling shows time spent waiting on WAL fsync. You now have a precise lever: synchronous_commit. Walk the ladder deliberately. For a queue of low-value events where losing the last 200 ms on a rare crash is acceptable, off can multiply throughput at essentially zero risk of corruption — only of a tiny, bounded loss of recent commits. For a payments table, you stay at on, or push to remote_apply so a committed charge is guaranteed present on a standby before you acknowledge it. The skill is not memorizing the settings; it's naming, per table or per transaction, how much recent-commit loss is tolerable — and Postgres even lets you set synchronous_commit per session or per transaction, so you can run most work fast and a few critical writes strict.

Step back and the WAL turns out to be the quiet foundation of the two biggest topics still ahead, because both are just "do something else with the same log":

  • Replication (Phase 8) ships the WAL byte-stream to standbys, which replay it — exactly the recovery loop you just watched, running live and forever. A standby is a crash-recovery machine that never finishes. Streaming replication, replication slots, and synchronous standbys are all built on top of this stream.
  • Point-in-time recovery (next lesson, L37) archives the WAL segments and lets you replay them on top of a base backup up to any chosen moment — for example, to one second before a bad DELETE. Same log, replayed selectively, to travel in time.

That is why this lesson comes before both. The WAL is not an operational footnote; it is the single artifact from which durability, replication, and recoverability all derive. If you understand that a committed transaction is the log record that was fsynced — and that data files, replicas, and backups are all just projections of that log — the rest of Phase 7 and Phase 8 stops being a list of features and becomes one idea seen from different angles.

Where this shows up next

★ Review

Summary & self-test

The whole lesson compresses to one idea: durability is bought by writing a cheap, sequential log of changes and fsyncing it at commit — the data pages follow lazily at checkpoints, and after a crash the database is rebuilt by replaying that log forward. Master that, and replication and PITR are the same trick reused.
