Failover, Consensus & Split-Brain

Replication gives you standbys — but a standby is worthless until, the instant the primary dies, one of them actually becomes the new primary. That promotion is failover, and doing it safely is harder than it looks: the real enemy is split-brain, two nodes both accepting writes and quietly diverging. This lesson shows how a cluster manager, a consensus store, and a proxy cooperate to promote exactly one standby — and how quorum and fencing guarantee at most one primary, ever.

Original study notes. Written for engineers who want database depth that pays off in interviews and in production incidents. Examples use PostgreSQL (PG 17/18) with Patroni, etcd, and HAProxy, but the ideas — leader leases, quorum, fencing — are how every distributed system elects a single leader. 00 Orientation

The primary just died — now what?

By now you know the shape of a replicated cluster (L42): one primary takes writes and streams its WAL to one or more standbys, and hot standbys can serve reads (L43 covered slots, lag, and synchronous replication). Replication buys you redundancy — but redundancy only pays out at the moment it matters: when the primary crashes, loses power, or falls off the network. A standby that never gets promoted is just an expensive backup.

The act of turning a standby into the new primary is failover. First, know which node you are even talking to — a standby is in recovery, a primary is not:

sql

-- Which node am I? true on a standby, false on the primary
SELECT pg_is_in_recovery();

-- Promote this standby to primary (what a manager runs under the hood)
SELECT pg_promote();

You could do failover by hand: an on-call engineer notices the primary is down, picks a healthy standby, runs pg_promote(), and repoints the application. That works, and it needs no extra infrastructure — but it is slow (minutes of downtime is a bad RTO, the recovery time objective) and error-prone at 3 a.m. Worse, a nervous human or a flaky script can promote a second standby while the first is still alive. Now you have two primaries.

Two questions turn out to be the entire subject:

  • Who decides to promote, and which standby — automatically, in seconds, without a human?
  • How do you guarantee only one primary exists at any instant — even during a network partition where the old primary is alive but simply unreachable?

That second question is the dangerous one. If the old primary is not really dead — just isolated — and it keeps accepting writes while a new primary is promoted elsewhere, the two databases diverge into two conflicting histories that cannot be cleanly merged. That is split-brain, and preventing it is what everything in this lesson is for.

The one invariant

Hold this line above everything else: at most one primary, ever. A cluster can briefly have zero primaries (a short write outage during failover, which the app rides out with retries) — that is survivable. It must never have two primaries taking writes. Every mechanism below — leader keys, quorum, fencing — exists to defend that single invariant.

01 The mechanism

Automatic failover: a leader key nobody can forge ▸

Automatic failover replaces the human with three cooperating pieces. First, a cluster manager — Patroni is the standard choice — runs as an agent beside each Postgres node, watching its health and driving promotion or demotion. Second, a consensus store, also called a DCS (distributed configuration store): etcd, Consul, or ZooKeeper, a small highly-available key/value cluster that is the single source of truth for one fact — who the primary is. Third, a proxy (HAProxy, or a VIP) that clients connect to, which always forwards writes to whichever node is currently the leader.

The trick that makes it safe is the leader key: a single key in the DCS, held with a short TTL lease. The primary must keep renewing that lease (a heartbeat). Holding the key is what makes a node the primary. Stop renewing — because you crashed — and the key expires, freeing the cluster to elect someone else. Step through the whole failover:

Notice what did the deciding: not the primary, and not any single standby — the DCS did, by refusing to hand the leader key to more than one node. Patroni is only the muscle that reacts to the key; etcd is the brain that holds the truth. That separation is the point. A node cannot promote itself just because it thinks the primary is gone; it must first win the key, and winning the key requires agreement from a majority of the DCS — which is exactly what the next section is about.

Holding the key == being primary

“Primary” is not a property baked into a server — it is a lease you currently hold in the DCS. This reframing kills a whole class of bugs: a node that lost the network can’t keep believing it is primary, because it can no longer renew the key. When the lease lapses, its own Patroni will demote it. The database’s identity lives in the consensus store, not in the box.

02 The math

Consensus & quorum: why you need an odd number ▸

How can etcd guarantee it never grants that leader key to two nodes at once — even while messages are being lost and nodes are crashing? The answer is a consensus algorithm (etcd uses Raft), and its core rule is quorum: no decision is made unless a majority of the cluster agrees on it. Concretely, quorum is ⌊N/2⌋ + 1 nodes — 2 of 3, 3 of 5, 4 of 7.

Majority is the magic number because two different majorities of the same set must overlap in at least one node — and that shared node will not vote for two conflicting leaders. So two leaders can never be elected at the same time. Watch a partition try, and fail, to produce a second leader:

Two consequences fall out of this, and both matter in practice. First, odd node counts: with an even cluster a partition can split evenly (2 | 2) and neither side has a majority, so nobody leads — you paid for a fourth node and got less fault tolerance, not more. Three nodes tolerate one failure; five tolerate two; four tolerate the same one as three. Second, this is why the DCS is a cluster in the first place: a single etcd node would be a single point of failure, and its “majority” would be trivially one node — no protection at all.

The DCS must survive, or failover can’t happen

Flip the guarantee around: if the etcd cluster itself loses quorum (say two of three DCS nodes die), then no leader key can be granted or renewed — and Postgres failover freezes. A healthy database cluster can be stuck read-only because its consensus store lost majority. That is why you run the DCS with its own odd, well-spread set of nodes and monitor it as carefully as the databases it governs.

03 The failure & the fix

Split-brain & fencing: at most one primary ▸

Quorum stops the minority side of a partition from electing a new leader. But it does not, by itself, stop the old primary — the one already running on the minority side — from continuing to accept writes it thinks are still valid. That gap is exactly where split-brain is born: the new primary and the stranded old primary both write, to two databases that now disagree. Watch it happen, then watch what closes the gap:

Fencing is the missing half. Quorum handles the majority side (only it can promote); fencing handles the minority side (the isolated old primary must be forced to stop writing). Patroni does this two ways at once. Because the old primary can no longer renew its leader key, its own Patroni agent demotes it (drops it to read-only). And for the terrifying case where Postgres is up but Patroni is wedged and can’t demote in time, a hardware or software watchdog (/dev/watchdog) reboots the machine if the agent fails to pet it within the TTL — a form of self-fencing (classic STONITH means a healthy node shoots the other; a watchdog is a node reliably shooting itself). Either way, the isolated node is out of the write path before it can diverge for longer than the lease.

Quorum and fencing are a pair

People memorize “quorum prevents split-brain” and it is half-true. Quorum guarantees the losing side can’t elect a new primary — but the losing side may already have the old one. You need fencing to positively stop that stranded primary. Config that makes fencing real: a required watchdog, a lease ttl short enough that the old primary self-demotes fast, and (for zero data loss) synchronous replication so an un-replicated write on the loser can’t have been acknowledged. The recovered node later rejoins as a standby with pg_rewind, snapping back onto the survivor’s timeline.

04 The stack

The real stack: Patroni, etcd, and HAProxy

In production the three roles are usually three named tools. It is worth seeing exactly how they wire together, because the config makes the abstract ideas concrete. Patroni runs on each database node and talks to the DCS:

yaml · patroni.yml

# one agent per Postgres node; all share the same DCS
scope: bank-cluster
etcd3:
  hosts: [10.0.0.11:2379, 10.0.0.12:2379, 10.0.0.13:2379]
bootstrap:
  dcs:
    ttl: 30            # leader-lease seconds: how long before a dead primary's key expires
    loop_wait: 10      # health-check / renewal interval
    retry_timeout: 10
postgresql:
  parameters:
    synchronous_commit: remote_apply   # only bites with synchronous_mode: true set (below)
watchdog:
  mode: required        # STONITH: reboot this node if the agent stalls

The DCS (etcd here) is a separate 3- or 5-node cluster holding the leader key and cluster state. Keep it on its own well-spread nodes; its health is your ability to fail over. Finally, HAProxy sends clients to whichever node is currently leader by probing Patroni’s REST health endpoint — only the true primary answers 200 on /primary:

haproxy.cfg

# writes go only to the current leader; the check does the routing
backend postgres_primary
    option httpchk GET /primary
    server pg1 10.0.0.11:5432 check port 8008
    server pg2 10.0.0.12:5432 check port 8008
    server pg3 10.0.0.13:5432 check port 8008

Two operations get confused constantly, so pin them down. A failover is unplanned: the primary died and the cluster reacts to save itself, possibly losing the last un-replicated writes (your RPO). A switchover is planned: you deliberately swap roles for maintenance, at a moment you choose, with zero data loss because the old primary is drained first. You drive a switchover yourself:

shell · patronictl

# planned, zero-data-loss role swap (for maintenance)
patronictl -c patroni.yml switchover --leader pg1 --candidate pg2

# inspect cluster state: who is Leader, who is Replica, and lag
patronictl -c patroni.yml list

And from inside SQL, the primary can see who is streaming and how far behind — the same lag that becomes your data loss if that node has to be promoted:

sql

-- run on the primary: replicas, sync status, and replay lag
SELECT client_addr, state, sync_state, replay_lag
FROM pg_stat_replication;

One last operational rule, and it is the one most teams skip: test your failover. A failover path that has never been exercised is a theory, not a safety net. Regularly (in staging, then production game-days) kill -9 the primary, pull its network cable, or stop its VM — and measure the real RTO while confirming exactly one primary comes back. The classic outage is discovering, during a real 3 a.m. incident, that the watchdog was never armed or the DCS had quietly lost quorum weeks ago.

05 Putting it together

Reason about it: the guarantee and its price

Step back and assemble the whole machine from the invariant down. The goal is at most one primary, ever. Achieving it needs three things working together, and you can now name why each is non-negotiable:

  • A single source of truth (the leader key in the DCS) so “who is primary” is one authoritative fact, not each node’s private opinion.
  • Quorum so that fact can only be changed by a majority — making it impossible for two sides of a partition to both claim leadership.
  • Fencing so a node that lost the truth is forcibly stopped from writing, closing the split-brain window the old primary would otherwise leave open.

Now see why the tempting shortcut is a disaster. A naive “just promote a standby” health-check script looks like this — and it is how real companies have corrupted real data:

shell · the dangerous version

# DON'T: promote from a single node's point of view
if ! pg_isready -h primary; then
    psql -h standby -c "SELECT pg_promote()"
fi
# Fatal flaw: "primary unreachable from HERE" is NOT "primary is dead".
# A network blip promotes the standby while the old primary keeps
# taking writes  ->  TWO primaries  ->  split-brain.
# No shared source of truth, no quorum, no fencing.

The script has no quorum (it trusts one observer’s ping), no shared truth (nothing stops a second promotion), and no fencing (it never stops the old primary). Every one of those is a place split-brain walks in. This is the lesson behind all the machinery: correct distributed failover is not a script, it is a protocol, and you should adopt Patroni/etcd rather than hand-roll it.

The guarantee also has a real price, and honesty about it is part of understanding it. You now run and must monitor a whole extra quorum system (etcd), whose own health gates your database’s ability to fail over — a lost DCS majority freezes promotion. Synchronous replication for zero data loss costs commit latency on every write. And even a perfect failover has a non-zero RTO: for a few seconds there is zero primary, in-flight connections drop, and writes error out. That is not a bug — it is the safe half of the invariant. It does mean the burden shifts to the application.

Where this shows up next

Because failover briefly breaks connections and rewinds a standby’s recent writes, your app cannot assume a connection lives forever or that a write always “took.” The database did its job — keep one primary; now the client must do its job: detect the drop, reconnect through the proxy, and retry idempotently. That is exactly the resilience work of L46–L48 (reconnect, retry, and read-after-write during failover). And it all builds on the replication foundation of L42 (topologies) and L43 (slots, lag, synchronous commit) — failover is what finally makes those standbys earn their keep.

★ Review

Summary & self-test

The whole lesson compresses to one idea: failover turns a standby into a primary the instant the old one dies — and the entire craft is guaranteeing that at most one primary ever exists, which takes a shared leader key, quorum to change it, and fencing to silence whoever lost it. Everything else — Patroni, etcd, HAProxy, watchdogs — is machinery in service of that single invariant.
