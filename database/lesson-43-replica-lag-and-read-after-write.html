Replica Lag & Read-After-Write

You add a read replica to take load off the primary, and within a day a support ticket arrives: "I updated my profile, the page reloaded, and it showed the old text — then I refreshed and it was fine." Nothing is broken; you just met replication lag. This practice lesson turns that intermittent ghost into something you can reason about and design away: the lag window, the three fixes, and a complete read-your-writes endpoint built on Postgres's own LSN functions.

43 Motivation

The read replica that lies to you

You are running out of read capacity on a single Postgres primary, so you add a hot standby read replica: writes still go to the primary, but reads fan out to the replica. Throughput jumps, the primary breathes, everyone is happy — for about a day. Then the ticket comes in: a user edited their profile, the app reloaded the page, and it showed the previous value. A second refresh fixed it. You cannot reproduce it on your laptop, where there is no replica at all.

The split is usually a two-pool setup in the app: writes and "must be fresh" reads on one connection string, scaled-out reads on another.

config

# app config — a read/write split
DATABASE_URL         = postgres://primary.db:5432/app   # writes + fresh reads
DATABASE_REPLICA_URL = postgres://replica.db:5432/app   # scaled-out reads

Here is the root cause. The user's UPDATE committed on the primary. The reload's SELECT was routed to the replica, which had not yet received and applied that commit. So the read returned a snapshot from a moment before the write — the user failed to see their own change. Refreshing worked because, by then, the replica had caught up.

Replication lag is the delay between a commit becoming durable on the primary and that change becoming visible to queries on a replica. With the default, fast, asynchronous streaming replication, that delay is never zero — usually a few milliseconds, but unbounded under load. This is not a Postgres defect; it is the CAP/PACELC trade you accepted when you chose async replication (availability and low latency, paid for in staleness). The engineering job is to make sure that staleness never produces a wrong answer a user actually notices.

The sharpest, most user-visible version is read-your-own-writes (a.k.a. read-after-write) consistency: after you successfully write X, any read you perform should see X or something newer. Note the scope — seeing someone else's edit a beat late is almost always fine; not seeing your own action is what makes an app feel broken.

The guarantees, ranked by strictness

Strong consistency (every read sees the latest write, from anyone) is what a single primary gives you and what a replica cannot without waiting. Read-your-writes is weaker but usually enough: your session sees your writes. Bounded staleness is weaker still: reads may be up to N milliseconds old. Eventual consistency is the floor: reads converge someday. This lesson is about buying exactly as much of this ladder as each read needs — and no more, because higher rungs cost primary load or latency.

43 The anomaly

The lag window, step by step ▸

To fix the bug you must see it precisely. Streaming replication moves a commit through four positions, each tracked as a Log Sequence Number (LSN) — a monotonically increasing address in the write-ahead log:

  • The commit is written to the primary's WAL and flushed (durable). The primary's write position is pg_current_wal_lsn().
  • The primary sends those WAL bytes to the standby (its sent_lsn).
  • The standby receives and flushes them to its own WAL (pg_last_wal_receive_lsn()).
  • The standby replays them into the data files (pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()). Only after replay is the change visible to queries on the replica.

The lag window is the gap between the commit on the primary and its replay on the replica. A read served by the replica inside that window returns a snapshot that predates your write. Watch one profile edit fall into the window:

Two things make this dangerous. First, the read simply has to land in the window — and right after a write, it very often does, because the user is looking at exactly the thing they just changed. Second, the window is usually milliseconds but is not bounded by default: a replica that is overloaded, stalled behind a long-running query, or cut off from the primary can fall seconds or minutes behind and keep serving very old data. Async replication is eventual consistency — the replica will converge, but "eventually" is a promise about the destination, not the wait.

Why replay is the line that matters

A replica can have received and even flushed your WAL to its disk while still serving stale reads, because queries only see changes after they are replayed into the data files. That is why read-your-writes checks target pg_last_wal_replay_lsn(), not the receive position. Replay can lag receive when a big query on the replica holds up recovery (with hot_standby_feedback and max_standby_streaming_delay governing that tug-of-war).

43 The options

Three fixes: primary, LSN-wait, bounded ▸

Every fix answers one question: after a write, where should this session's next read go? There are three good answers, and they trade correctness against cost differently. Step through them:

1 · Route to primary

  • Send reads-after-writes to the primary for a short window.
  • Always correct — the primary has everything.
  • Cost: primary load. Too broad a window and the replica is wasted.

2 · LSN-wait

  • Capture the write's LSN; wait for the replica to replay past it, then read it.
  • Correct and keeps the read off the primary.
  • Cost: a small wait + a timeout fallback; more plumbing.

3 · Bounded staleness

  • Accept data up to N ms old; read the replica only if its lag is under the bound.
  • Cheapest, highest offload.
  • Cost: not read-your-writes — only for reads that tolerate a little staleness.

These are not a global switch you flip once. You choose per read: the profile the user just edited earns fix 1 or 2; a public "trending" list is happy with fix 3; an unrelated dashboard chart may not care at all. A common, healthy default is "replica with bounded staleness for most reads, LSN-wait or primary for the handful that just followed a write."

The instinct to build

Read-your-writes is a session guarantee, not a database mode. Postgres will not do it for you across a primary and its replicas — your routing layer decides. So the question at every read site is: "did this session just write something it now expects to see?" If yes, use fix 1 or 2. If no, the replica is free game. Getting that one question right at each endpoint is the whole discipline.

43 Patterns

Patterns, plumbing & watching the lag

Two implementation styles cover almost every real system, and they map onto fixes 1 and 2 above.

Sticky routing (coarse, simple)

Record a "last wrote at" timestamp (or the last LSN) per session. For a window after any write — say 5 seconds — route all of that session's reads to the primary; after the window, fall back to the replica. It needs no LSN threading and no client cooperation, so it is the easiest to add. The costs: it is coarse (every read in the window hits the primary, even ones that did not need to), and the "session" state must be shared across all your app instances — a signed cookie, or a small shared store — so a user's next request, which may hit a different server, still sees "you just wrote."

LSN token passing (precise, scalable)

On a write, return the write's LSN to the client as a token (a response header or cookie). The client echoes it on subsequent reads; the read layer waits for the replica to replay past that LSN before serving, and falls back to the primary on timeout. This is precise — it pins consistency to the specific write, not a blunt time window — and it scales, because most reads carry no token and go straight to a replica. The price is plumbing: you must thread the token through your API and clients.

Watch the lag, and cap it

On the primary, pg_stat_replication shows one row per connected standby, including a ready-made replay_lag interval and the LSNs to diff:

sql

-- run on the PRIMARY: how far behind is each standby?
SELECT application_name, state, sync_state,
       replay_lag,
       pg_wal_lsn_diff(sent_lsn, replay_lsn) AS bytes_behind
FROM   pg_stat_replication;

text · illustrative

 application_name | state     | sync_state | replay_lag      | bytes_behind
------------------+-----------+------------+-----------------+--------------
 replica1         | streaming | async      | 00:00:00.012    |         8192
 replica2         | streaming | async      | 00:00:04.361    |      6553600

On a replica, ask it directly how current it is — by LSN and by wall-clock:

sql

-- run on the REPLICA
SELECT pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()                               AS replayed,
       now() - pg_last_xact_replay_timestamp()               AS time_behind;

Alert on both a byte threshold and a time threshold, and remember the classic operational disaster from the replication lesson: an inactive replication slot makes the primary retain WAL forever, filling its disk, while a paused replica keeps happily serving month-old reads. Bounded staleness is not just a routing rule — it is an alert.

Two more session guarantees worth a name

Monotonic reads: a session should never see time run backwards (value present on one read, gone on the next because two reads hit two replicas at different lag). Pin a session to one replica, or track the max LSN it has already seen and never serve below it. Causal consistency: if write B depended on reading A, anyone who sees B must also see A. Both fall out naturally from LSN tracking — the token you pass for read-your-writes is the same tool. The one-line takeaway for the whole lesson: a replica is never guaranteed current; design each read for the staleness it can tolerate.

43 Worked build

Build a read-your-writes endpoint ▸

Time to build the real thing: the "update profile, then show it" flow, done so the user always sees their own edit — without simply firing every read at the primary. First, watch the LSN-wait handshake end to end: the write captures a token, the read carries it back, the replica waits until it has replayed past it, and a timeout falls back to the primary.

1 · The schema & setup

A minimal profiles table on the primary, plus the app's two connection pools from section 00. Nothing about the schema is special — read-your-writes lives entirely in the routing layer.

sql

-- on the PRIMARY (replicated physically to the standby)
CREATE TABLE profiles (
  user_id  bigint PRIMARY KEY,
  bio      text   NOT NULL,
  updated_at timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

2 · The task

Build two handlers: POST /profile writes the new bio; GET /profile renders it. The user must always see their own just-saved bio on the reload, but ordinary browsing traffic should still be free to hit the replica. Write (a) the naive version and say why it fails, then (b) the LSN-wait fix.

3 · The naive version, and why it is wrong

The obvious split — writes to the primary, all reads to the replica — is exactly the bug:

sql

-- POST /profile  (on the PRIMARY connection)
UPDATE profiles SET bio = $1, updated_at = now() WHERE user_id = $2;

-- GET /profile  (on the REPLICA connection) — WRONG
-- If this runs inside the lag window, it returns the OLD bio:
-- the replica has not replayed the UPDATE yet.
SELECT bio FROM profiles WHERE user_id = $2;

The UPDATE is durable on the primary, but the very next SELECT asks a replica that may still be a few milliseconds behind. The user reads their old bio and files the ticket. Sending all reads to the primary would fix correctness but throw away the replica — we want the read on the replica and correct.

4 · The fix: capture the LSN, wait for it, fall back

On the write, capture the primary's current WAL position — a value at or past our commit's LSN — and hand it back as a token. On the read, wait for the replica to replay up to that token, then read the replica; if it does not catch up in time, read the primary instead.

sql

-- POST /profile  (on the PRIMARY): write, then capture the LSN token
UPDATE profiles SET bio = $1, updated_at = now() WHERE user_id = $2;
SELECT pg_current_wal_lsn();   -- e.g. '0/3A2F108' -> return to client as a token

sql

-- GET /profile  (on the REPLICA): has it replayed past the token yet?
-- pg_lsn compares directly; returns a boolean.
SELECT pg_last_wal_replay_lsn() >= $1::pg_lsn AS caught_up;

The read handler polls that boolean with a short sleep until it is true or a deadline passes, then chooses its connection. In app pseudocode:

text

token = request.cookie("wlsn")            # the LSN from the last write, if any
conn  = replica
if token:
    deadline = now() + 200ms
    while now() < deadline:
        if replica.query("SELECT pg_last_wal_replay_lsn() >= $1::pg_lsn", token):
            break                         # replica is current enough — read it
        sleep(10ms)
    else:
        conn = primary                    # timed out: fall back to the primary
bio = conn.query("SELECT bio FROM profiles WHERE user_id = $1", user_id)

5 · Why it is better

Every read-your-writes read is now correct: either the replica has provably replayed past your write, or you read the primary. Yet the common case — no token, or a replica that is already current — reads the replica immediately, so the primary offload survives. An illustrative psql session on the replica shows the boolean flipping as recovery advances:

text · illustrative

-- moments after the commit, replica still catching up:
replica=# SELECT pg_last_wal_replay_lsn(),
replica-#        pg_last_wal_replay_lsn() >= '0/3A2F108'::pg_lsn AS caught_up;
 pg_last_wal_replay_lsn | caught_up
------------------------+-----------
 0/3A2E9C0              | f            <-- not yet: wait / poll

-- a few milliseconds later:
 pg_last_wal_replay_lsn | caught_up
------------------------+-----------
 0/3A2F1A0              | t            <-- replayed past the token: read the replica

Two correctness notes. pg_current_wal_lsn() must be called on the primary — it raises "recovery is in progress" on a standby, where you would use pg_last_wal_receive_lsn() instead. And capturing the WAL position right after COMMIT returns a value at or beyond your commit record, so a replica that has replayed to the token has, by construction, replayed your write.

Narrate it in an interview

"Writes go to the primary; after each write I capture pg_current_wal_lsn() and hand it to the client as a token. On a read, if a token is present I wait — up to a small deadline — for the replica's pg_last_wal_replay_lsn() to reach it, then read the replica; if it does not catch up in time I read the primary. Reads with no token skip all of this and go straight to a replica. That gives per-write read-your-writes consistency while keeping almost all read traffic off the primary." The same LSN-token idea powers monotonic reads and causal consistency — it is the one tool worth mastering here.

43 Drills

Graded drills & traps

Attempt each drill before opening its answer. They climb from a lag readout to full sticky routing.

Drill 1 — Measure how far a replica is behind

Running on the replica, report how many bytes of WAL it has received but not yet replayed, and how old its most recent replayed transaction is.

Try it first, then read

Diff the receive and replay LSNs for the byte gap, and subtract the last replay timestamp from now() for the time gap:

SELECT pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_last_wal_receive_lsn(),
                       pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()) AS replay_lag_bytes,
       now() - pg_last_xact_replay_timestamp()   AS replay_lag_time;

Why: received-minus-replayed is the work still queued in recovery; the timestamp gap is the user-facing staleness. Track both.

Drill 2 — A bounded-staleness router

For a read that tolerates up to 500 ms of staleness, write the replica-side check that decides "fresh enough to serve here."

Try it first, then read

Compare the replica's time-behind to the bound; if under it, serve the replica, else fall back to the primary:

SELECT now() - pg_last_xact_replay_timestamp()
         < interval '500 milliseconds' AS fresh_enough;

Why: this is time-bounded staleness, not read-your-writes — fine for a feed, wrong for the user's own edit. Beware idle periods: with no writes, the replay timestamp stops advancing and can read as "stale" though the replica is fully current.

Drill 3 — The LSN-wait guard, safely

Write the exact replica-side boolean that guards a read-your-writes read against a token LSN — and name the one call that must not run on a standby.

Try it first, then read

The guard compares the replayed LSN to the token; the forbidden call on a standby is pg_current_wal_lsn() (capture the token on the primary at write time):

-- on the REPLICA: safe read-your-writes guard
SELECT pg_last_wal_replay_lsn() >= $1::pg_lsn AS caught_up;
-- pg_current_wal_lsn() here raises "recovery is in progress"

Why: pg_lsn compares as a real ordering, so no string math — '0/9' < '0/10' is true as pg_lsn but false as text.

Drill 4 — Sticky read-your-writes without tokens

Some clients cannot echo a token (a plain browser form). Give that session read-your-writes anyway by pinning its reads to the primary for 5 seconds after its last write. Sketch the routing decision.

Try it first, then read

Stamp a per-session last_write_at on every write (in a shared store or signed cookie), and route based on the window:

# on write:  session.last_write_at = now()
if session.last_write_at and now() - session.last_write_at < 5s:
    conn = primary        # sticky window: read-your-writes
else:
    conn = replica        # window elapsed: replica is fine

Why: coarser than LSN-wait (all reads in the window hit the primary) but needs no client cooperation. The window must be shared across app instances, or a request landing on another server skips the stickiness.

The traps that turn lag into an outage

(1) Reading your own write from a replica. The core bug: writes to the primary, the immediate read to a lagging replica. Route reads-after-writes to the primary or LSN-wait them.
(2) Unbounded staleness. A stalled or slot-pinned replica serves data minutes old with no cap. Alert on both byte-lag and time-lag; never trust a replica to be current.
(3) Mishandling LSNs. Comparing them as strings, confusing receive vs replay, or calling pg_current_wal_lsn() on a standby. Use the pg_lsn type and the right function for the node.
(4) Sticky windows that leak. A too-long window (or one not shared across servers) either floods the primary or silently fails to stick — measure and bound it.
(5) Time-only lag on an idle system. With no writes, pg_last_xact_replay_timestamp() freezes and looks stale though the replica is caught up. Combine with the LSN byte diff.
(6) Sending a write to a replica. A misrouted UPDATE hits a read-only standby and errors "cannot execute UPDATE in a read-only transaction." Keep writes strictly on the primary pool.

★ Review

Summary & self-test

The whole lesson compresses to one line: async replication makes a replica eventually consistent, so a read inside the lag window can miss your own write — and you fix it per read by routing to the primary, waiting on the replica's replayed LSN, or bounding the staleness you accept. Everything else is plumbing around those three choices.
