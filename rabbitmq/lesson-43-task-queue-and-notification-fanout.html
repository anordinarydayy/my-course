Build: Design a Task Queue & Notification Fan-Out

Two designs cover most of what interviewers actually ask you to draw with RabbitMQ: a reliable background task queue, and a notification fan-out to independently operated channels. Neither delivers exactly-once. Both are honest, defensible, at-least-once designs — and the failure ledger for the first one is the artifact that shows you've actually thought through what happens when something breaks.

Original study chapter. The topologies, invariants, and code are written from scratch. Stable behavior targets open-source RabbitMQ 4.3, with the signed 4.3.3 maintenance release as the current patch overlay; runnable code uses Python 3 and Pika 1.3.2. This chapter contains three original figures, two interactive.
00 Framing

The two designs interviewers actually ask you to draw

“Design a reliable job queue” and “design a notification system that fans out to email, SMS, and push” are two of the most common RabbitMQ system-design prompts, and they exercise almost every mechanism from earlier phases at once: durability, confirms, quorum queues, prefetch, retry, dead-lettering, and exchange choice. Neither design promises more than it can deliver — both are stated up front as at-least-once, with the duplicate-handling burden placed explicitly on the consumer, not hand-waved away.

The core distinction

Design A optimizes for one workload, reliably processed once each, with an explicit failure ledger. Design B optimizes for one event, independently delivered to several different channels, each with its own failure isolation. Confusing the two shapes — sharing one queue across channels, or fanning a task queue out to competing workers as if they were independent subscribers — is the most common mistake in a live design round.

01 Design A

Topology: a durable exchange to a quorum queue ▸

Workload statement: important asynchronous jobs; duplicate attempts are acceptable only when made harmless; bounded processing latency and backlog; no requirement for global order across all jobs.

A durable exchange routes to a durable quorum queue for important work — quorum because replicated data safety is required here, not because every temporary or minimum-latency queue should default to quorum. A classic queue remains valid when replication isn't required, and a stream fits a retained, replay-heavy backlog instead. The queue carries explicit message and byte limits with an intentional overflow response, and the retry/parking topology is declared up front, not bolted on after the first incident.

Producer invariants: publish persistent messages with a stable job/idempotency ID and enough metadata to classify age and attempts; use publisher confirms asynchronously or in bounded batches and track unresolved sequence numbers across a connection failure, since retrying an unconfirmed publish can duplicate; use mandatory=true plus a return handler (or a deliberate alternate exchange) when “must route to a queue” is part of the contract, because a positive confirm alone does not prove a route existed; prefer long-lived connections and channels rather than publish-and-wait per message; and if a database commit and the publication form one business invariant, use a transactional outbox and relay rather than pretending a confirm creates a database-plus-RabbitMQ transaction.

02 Design A

Worker invariants: commit, then acknowledge ▸

Use a long-lived push consumer with manual acknowledgements and a measured prefetch bound. Validate and classify the job before expensive work — permanent failures go directly to terminal handling, transient failures enter the bounded delay policy. Commit the externally visible side effect and the idempotency record atomically where the target store permits, then acknowledge; a crash after commit but before ack is a normal duplicate window, not a bug. Treat the redelivered flag and broker death counters as diagnostics, never as unique IDs or proof of prior completion. Scale competing workers until the queue, downstream service, or the one-queue hot path becomes the measured constraint — prefetch and worker count are separate controls, and neither one alone fixes the other.

For retry and terminal handling, use RabbitMQ 4.3 quorum delayed retry when its capped-linear model fits, or TTL-plus-DLX tiers when portability or distinct delay tiers matter more. Enforce attempt and age limits independently of basic.nack, because nack returns do not advance quorum delivery-count in 4.3. Park exhausted jobs in declared, monitored topology and replay only through a rate-limited, idempotent tool — never by hand, and never silently.

The honest guarantee statement

Design A supports at-least-once processing attempts under its stated confirm, durability, quorum, acknowledgement, and retry assumptions. It does not provide exactly-once external side effects, and it does not provide an unconditional no-loss guarantee. Say this out loud in an interview — it's the sentence that separates a defensible design from an overclaimed one.

04 Design B

Notification fan-out: a topic exchange, one queue per channel

Workload statement: one logical notification event may need email, SMS, push, and audit — independently scaled channels; routing depends on event type and policy; provider calls can duplicate or fail; long-term user inbox state belongs outside a transient delivery queue.

Bind one durable queue per independently operated channel, never one shared queue for all channels. Email workers compete within the email queue; SMS workers compete within the SMS queue — this preserves fan-out between channels while keeping ordinary work-sharing inside each one. Use fanout only when every bound channel genuinely needs every event; topic or direct routing avoids copying irrelevant work to a channel that doesn't need it.

05 Design B

Channel invariants and the queue-per-user nuance

Give each channel its own quorum-or-classic choice, prefetch, concurrency, rate limits, timeout, bounded retry schedule, and terminal parking path — a slow SMS provider must never block email delivery, and sharing infrastructure between channels is exactly how that coupling creeps back in. Keep user preferences, templates, provider credentials, suppression rules, and authoritative notification history in application storage, resolved before routing or inside an idempotent channel processor. Make provider calls idempotent where supported; otherwise persist a stable provider request key and reconcile uncertain outcomes — a consumer acknowledgement happens only after the channel's durable success record is committed, mirroring Design A's commit-then-ack rule exactly.

For a very high-cardinality offline inbox or a long replay horizon, consider database inbox rows or a retained stream rather than a durable replicated queue per user — queue-per-user remains a workload decision, not a categorical error, the same nuance from the anti-pattern register two lessons back. The AWS-native analogue is SNS fan-out to one SQS queue per independently processed subscriber; it demonstrates the same independent-buffer principle, but it is not evidence that SNS/SQS and RabbitMQ share identical delivery or operational semantics.

06 Build

Declare Design A's topology end to end

Exchange, quorum queue with delayed retry, and a DLXPython 3 · Pika 1.3.2

Declare the topology and publish one confirmed, idempotency-tagged job

python · install pika==1.3.2 and run against a RabbitMQ 4.3 node
import uuid

import pika

EXCHANGE = "jobs.topic"
QUEUE = f"jobs.export.{uuid.uuid4().hex[:8]}"
PARKING = f"{QUEUE}.parking"

connection = pika.BlockingConnection(pika.ConnectionParameters("localhost"))
channel = connection.channel()
try:
    channel.exchange_declare(exchange=EXCHANGE, exchange_type="topic", durable=True)

    # Terminal parking queue for exhausted jobs — declared explicitly, monitored.
    channel.queue_declare(queue=PARKING, durable=True)

    # Quorum queue: replicated safety, capped-linear delayed retry, explicit limits.
    channel.queue_declare(
        queue=QUEUE,
        durable=True,
        arguments={
            "x-queue-type": "quorum",
            "x-delivery-limit": 5,                 # bounded attempts, independent of nack
            "x-dead-letter-exchange": "",           # default exchange -> route by queue name
            "x-dead-letter-routing-key": PARKING,
            "x-max-length": 100000,                 # explicit overload guardrail
            "x-overflow": "reject-publish",
        },
    )
    channel.queue_bind(queue=QUEUE, exchange=EXCHANGE, routing_key="jobs.export.*")
    channel.confirm_delivery()

    job_id = str(uuid.uuid4())  # the idempotency ID a worker's side-effect record keys on
    channel.basic_publish(
        exchange=EXCHANGE,
        routing_key="jobs.export.csv",
        body=job_id.encode(),
        properties=pika.BasicProperties(delivery_mode=2, message_id=job_id),
        mandatory=True,  # raises on nack/return in Pika's blocking client — reaching past this line IS the confirm
    )
    print({"published_job_id": job_id, "queue": QUEUE, "parking": PARKING})
finally:
    channel.queue_delete(queue=QUEUE)
    channel.queue_delete(queue=PARKING)
    connection.close()

The delivery limit and the dead-letter arguments are declared on the queue itself, not left to be discovered after the first poison message — that declaration is the retry and terminal-path invariant from Design A made concrete. Notice that reaching the line after basic_publish is the confirm in Pika's blocking client; a real production publisher would still track unresolved sequence numbers across a connection failure rather than relying on a single synchronous call per message.

Seven takeaways

Self-test

1. Why is Design A's queue a quorum queue specifically?

Because the work is important and replicated data safety is required — not because every queue should default to quorum. Classic queues remain valid when replication isn't needed.

2. What's the observable difference between a worker crashing before versus after committing its side effect?

Before commit: the delivery is simply redelivered with no side effect having happened — safe. After commit but before ack: a genuine duplicate attempt that only an idempotency record can absorb safely.

3. Does a positive publisher confirm prove the message was routed to a queue?

No. Confirm and routing answer different questions — an unroutable message can still be positively confirmed unless mandatory/return handling or an alternate exchange is also in place.

4. Why does Design B use one queue per channel instead of one shared queue?

To isolate failure and pacing per channel — a shared queue would let a slow or failing SMS provider block email delivery, defeating the purpose of independent channels.

5. Is queue-per-user always an anti-pattern in Design B's notification use case?

No. It's a workload decision based on cardinality, churn, and replay needs — valid at bounded scale, while very high cardinality or long replay favors database rows or a stream instead.

6. What is Design A's honest guarantee statement?

At-least-once processing attempts under its stated confirm, durability, quorum, acknowledgement, and retry assumptions — never exactly-once external side effects, and never an unconditional no-loss guarantee.
