Network Partitions & Split-Brain

A network partition used to be a configuration question in RabbitMQ — pick pause_minority or autoheal and hope. In 4.3 it is a consensus question with one answer: the side with a majority continues, the minority cannot commit, and the old knobs do nothing. This lesson replaces partition-mode folklore with the Raft rules that actually govern a split — and shows why “majority of which group?” is the question that matters.

Original study chapter. The scenarios, diagrams, code, and SVGs are written from scratch. Stable behavior targets open-source RabbitMQ 4.3, with the signed 4.3.3 maintenance release as the current patch overlay; runnable code uses Python 3 and Pika 1.3.2. This chapter contains three original figures, two interactive. Legacy partition-handling settings are accepted but have no effect in 4.3.
00 Failure first

Setting cluster_partition_handling in 4.3 does nothing

An engineer hardens a cluster for split-brain the way they always have: they set cluster_partition_handling = pause_minority, restart, and consider it handled. It is not handled — that setting is accepted for compatibility but has no effect in RabbitMQ 4.3. The knob still parses, so nothing warns them, and they carry a false belief into their next outage. Meanwhile a teammate worries about the opposite failure: “won’t a partition split-brain my quorum queue and let both sides accept conflicting writes?”

Both anxieties dissolve once you see what changed. In 4.3 the components that hold replicated state — the Khepri metadata store, every quorum queue, and stream coordinators — all run on Raft. Raft has exactly one rule for a partition: only a side that can assemble a majority may commit. There is no configurable mode because the behavior is not a policy; it is arithmetic. The minority cannot commit conflicting state, so a quorum queue cannot “split-brain” into two divergent copies.

So the old mental model — choose a partition strategy, worry about corruption — is replaced by a sharper one: identify each Raft group, ask which side of the split holds its majority, and know that the other side simply pauses its consensus operations until the network heals. The rest of this lesson makes that concrete, including the subtle case where two groups have their majorities on different sides at once.

The core distinction

A partition is resolved by Raft majority, not by a setting. The majority side of each group continues; the minority cannot commit. Old cluster_partition_handling / pause_if_all_down keys are accepted but inert — remove them.

01 Mechanism

When the network splits, the majority continues ▸

Picture a three-node cluster whose network splits into a two-node side and a one-node side. Each Raft group on that cluster now asks the same question independently: can I still see a majority of my members? For a group whose members are all three nodes, the two-node side answers yes. It elects or retains a leader and keeps committing. The one-node side answers no; it cannot commit new consensus operations, and during any leaderless interval writes are rejected or delayed even on the majority side until a leader is settled.

Crucially, this is not corruption and not divergence. The minority does not accept conflicting committed writes that would later have to be merged or discarded — it simply cannot commit them. That is the whole reason RabbitMQ removed the old “choose your poison” partition modes: Raft makes the safe choice unconditionally. 02 Invariant

The old partition modes are removed, not merely superseded

Be exact about the status of the legacy settings, because “superseded” understates it. The Mnesia-era strategies — ignore, autoheal, pause_minority, and pause_if_all_down — were removed. In 4.3 the related configuration keys are accepted for compatibility but have no effect whatsoever, and the guidance is to delete them from your configuration. There is no replacement knob, because Raft majority behavior is not something you configure.

This is where a couple of 4.3 documentation pages can mislead: the quorum and clustering pages still contain isolated references to configurable partition modes. Those are stale carry-over. The tagged 4.3.0 release notes and the reworked partitions guide are authoritative and explicitly remove the strategies. When a page and the release notes disagree, the release notes win.

The payoff of the removal is the guarantee you wanted all along. Because the minority cannot commit, a partition cannot produce two divergent, individually-committed versions of a quorum queue or of the metadata. There is nothing to “heal” in the Mnesia sense of choosing a survivor and discarding the loser’s writes; the loser never committed conflicting writes in the first place.

Configuration warning

Delete cluster_partition_handling and pause_if_all_down from 4.3 configuration. Leaving them in place gives a false sense of control — they parse without error but change nothing. Design for Raft majority instead.

03 Boundary

Majority of which group? Khepri and a queue can differ ▸

The single most important subtlety in a partition is that different Raft groups have different membership, so they can have their majorities on different sides at the same instant. Khepri’s members are the broker cluster nodes; a quorum queue’s members are only the nodes it was placed on. A five-node cluster commonly hosts a three-member queue — so a split can leave Khepri’s majority on one side and that queue’s majority on the other.

Work the example. A five-node cluster splits into {A,B} and {C,D,E}. Khepri counts broker nodes: three of five are on the {C,D,E} side, so metadata commits there. A queue whose members are A, B, and C counts only those: two of its three members (A and B) are on the {A,B} side, so the queue’s leader and commits are there. Metadata and that queue now have majorities on opposite sides — both correct, because each evaluates its own membership. The stepper makes this split-of-scopes concrete.

04 Mechanism

The minority pauses consensus — it is not simply switched off

“The minority is down” is a convenient shorthand that hides useful behavior. On the disconnected side, Khepri can still serve local cached reads, so clients querying already-known metadata are not universally rejected. Quorum-queue deliveries on the minority side pause, because delivery needs the leader and a majority. Stream consumers can still read data that was already replicated locally. What the minority cannot do is commit anything that requires consensus — new topology, new quorum-queue writes that need a majority.

Recovery is equally undramatic. When connectivity returns, a minority member rejoins its group as a follower and catches up the log from the current leader; it is not eligible to lead until it has caught up. The operator’s job is to observe member state and catch-up progress, not to intervene as in the Mnesia era. Specifically, you do not restart both sides to “resync,” and you do not configure a winning partition — those actions belonged to a model RabbitMQ has left behind.

05 Trade-off

Odd counts, the two-node reality, and the diagnostics that tell the truth

Because everything hinges on majorities, node and member counts should be odd, and the failure-tolerance arithmetic is worth memorising: three tolerate one loss, five tolerate two, and two tolerate zero. That last figure is the honest version of the old “never run two nodes” slogan. Two nodes are a supported topology; they are simply strongly discouraged for consensus workloads because a single node or link failure leaves no majority, so the whole group stalls. “Zero fault tolerance,” not “forbidden,” is the precise claim.

During and after a partition, read reality from the broker instead of guessing. These are all read-only, and none of them configure a “winner” — that concept no longer exists.

06 Build

Encode the arithmetic a partition obeys, then watch a majority commit

You cannot cut a network cable from a Python script, but you can make the governing arithmetic executable and then confirm the one observable fact it predicts: a publisher confirm only comes back when a majority commits. The pure functions assert the tolerance table; the live publish asserts that a confirmed write really did commit — the exact acknowledgement a partitioned minority could never produce.

Partition-majority checkpointPython 3 · Pika 1.3.2

Assert the majority arithmetic, then prove a majority commit with a confirm

python · install pika==1.3.2 and run against localhost
import uuid

import pika


def majority(members):
    return members // 2 + 1


def failures_tolerated(members):
    return members - majority(members)


# The arithmetic a partition obeys: only a side with a majority may commit.
assert majority(3) == 2 and failures_tolerated(3) == 1
assert majority(5) == 3 and failures_tolerated(5) == 2
assert failures_tolerated(2) == 0                          # two members tolerate zero
assert failures_tolerated(4) == failures_tolerated(3)      # a 4th vote adds no tolerance

QUEUE = f"lab.l30.split.{uuid.uuid4().hex[:10]}"
connection = pika.BlockingConnection(pika.ConnectionParameters("localhost"))
channel = connection.channel()
try:
    channel.queue_declare(
        queue=QUEUE,
        durable=True,
        arguments={"x-queue-type": "quorum", "x-quorum-initial-group-size": 3},
    )
    channel.confirm_delivery()

    # A returned confirm means a MAJORITY committed the write. A partitioned
    # minority cannot produce this ack, which is exactly why it cannot take work.
    channel.basic_publish(
        exchange="",
        routing_key=QUEUE,
        body=b"committed-by-majority",
        properties=pika.BasicProperties(delivery_mode=2),
        mandatory=True,
    )
    ready = channel.queue_declare(queue=QUEUE, passive=True).method.message_count
    assert ready == 1
    print({
        "tolerate_3": failures_tolerated(3),
        "tolerate_5": failures_tolerated(5),
        "ready": ready,
    })
finally:
    channel.queue_delete(queue=QUEUE)
    connection.close()

The arithmetic is the whole story of a partition: change the member counts and you change which side keeps working. The confirmed publish is the observable proof that a majority committed — and the thing the isolated minority can never do.

Self-test

1. What does cluster_partition_handling do in RabbitMQ 4.3?

Nothing functional. The Mnesia-era partition strategies were removed; the keys are accepted for compatibility but have no effect. Raft majority behavior is not configurable, so the setting should be deleted.

2. Can a partition split-brain a quorum queue into two conflicting copies?

No. Only a side with a majority of the queue’s members can commit, so the minority side cannot accept conflicting committed writes. There is nothing to merge or discard on heal.

3. Why can Khepri and a quorum queue have majorities on different sides?

Because they have different membership. Khepri’s members are all broker nodes; a quorum queue’s members are only the nodes it was placed on. A split can leave each group’s majority on a different side.

4. Is the minority side of a partition simply down?

No. Khepri can serve cached reads there, and stream consumers can read locally replicated data; quorum-queue deliveries pause. What the minority cannot do is commit operations that require consensus.

5. Should you ever run a two-node cluster for HA?

It is a supported topology but strongly discouraged for consensus workloads: two members have a majority of two and tolerate zero failures, so any single failure stalls the group. Prefer odd counts like three or five.

6. What do you do when the network heals?

Observe member state and catch-up: each returning member rejoins as a follower and replays the log from its group’s current leader. Do not restart both sides or configure a winning partition — that was the Mnesia era.
