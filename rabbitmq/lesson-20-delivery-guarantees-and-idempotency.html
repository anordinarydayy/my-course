Delivery Guarantees & Idempotency

Acknowledgements and confirms buy at-least-once behavior for the hops they cover, and at-least-once always admits duplicates. This lesson defines the guarantees precisely per hop, locates the two windows where a duplicate is born, and pushes the fix where it belongs: an idempotent consumer that commits its dedup check in the same transaction as its work.

Original study chapter. The scenarios, diagrams, code, and SVGs are written from scratch. Stable behavior targets open-source RabbitMQ 4.3 (latest signed patch overlay 4.3.3); runnable code uses Python 3, Pika 1.3.2, and the standard-library sqlite3. This chapter contains three original figures, two interactive. Idempotency is an application invariant, not a RabbitMQ feature.
00 Failure first

“Exactly-once delivery” is the wrong thing to reach for

Teams often set out to build “exactly-once” message processing, wire up confirms and acknowledgements, and assume the problem is solved. It is not, and the reason is worth stating carefully rather than with a slogan. RabbitMQ’s reliability guide summarises acknowledged operation as at least once and unacknowledged operation as at most once. Neither is exactly-once, and the precise, defensible statement is this: RabbitMQ confirms and consumer acknowledgements alone do not provide exactly-once business processing across an external side effect.

That is narrower and more useful than “exactly-once is a myth.” The broker mechanisms are real and strong; they simply operate on network hops, and a message workflow crosses several of them plus your database, your HTTP calls, and your email sends. A guarantee that holds on one hop does not automatically extend to a business outcome that spans all of them.

So the engineering move is not to chase a guarantee the tools do not offer. It is to attach the right label—at-most-once or at-least-once—to each named hop, accept that at-least-once means duplicates, and design the consumer so a duplicate is harmless. The rest of this lesson does exactly that.

Precise framing

Attach a guarantee to a hop and its conditions, not to a whole workflow. “At least once for RabbitMQ→consumer, given manual acks and requeue” is a claim you can defend. “Exactly-once” for the end-to-end business result is not.

01 Mechanism

At-most-once loses; at-least-once duplicates — per hop

The two labels are a trade, and you pick a side per hop based on which failure is cheaper. At-most-once means each message is handled zero or one times: automatic consumer acknowledgement and fire-and-forget publishing avoid broker-driven duplicates, but a failure at the wrong moment loses the message with no retry. It is a legitimate choice for best-effort data; it is not “more reliable because we saw no duplicates.” No duplicates observed is not the same as no loss.

At-least-once means each message is handled one or more times: retrying unconfirmed publishes and requeueing unacknowledged deliveries close the loss windows, at the cost of opening duplicate windows. A publisher can lose a confirm after RabbitMQ accepted a message and republish it. A consumer can commit its database transaction and then crash before its ack lands, so RabbitMQ redelivers and the work runs again. Both are inherent, not bugs.

Crucially, “at least once” is not free with a checkbox. It requires the retransmission or requeue behavior plus the relevant persistence, topology, and failure assumptions. A durable queue with persistent messages and confirms gives at-least-once across a broker restart; a quorum queue binds confirms to quorum acceptance—but only while the required majority holds, and an unconfirmed message has no such guarantee at all. The label always travels with its conditions.

02 Boundary

Two ambiguity windows, on two different hops ▸

Duplicates are not random; they come from two specific windows, each on its own hop. The first is on producer→RabbitMQ: the broker accepts and persists a message, then the confirm is lost before the producer sees it. The producer, to honor at-least-once, republishes—and now the broker holds two copies. The second is on RabbitMQ→consumer: the consumer does its durable work, commits, and then crashes or loses the connection before the ack. RabbitMQ requeues and redelivers, and the work runs a second time.

Neither window can be closed by a broker feature, because in both cases the failure falls between two systems that cannot commit atomically together—the broker and the producer’s knowledge, or the consumer’s database and the broker’s acknowledgement. The stepper walks both windows on the same timeline so you can see that they are distinct hops with the same shape: a durable effect on one side, a lost signal on the other, and a retry that duplicates.

03 Invariant

Acks and confirms are not atomic with your side effect

Here is the invariant that survives every configuration: a RabbitMQ acknowledgement is not atomic with an arbitrary database transaction, HTTP call, email send, or other external effect. There is no way to make “commit the order” and “send the ack” a single all-or-nothing operation across two systems, so one of them always happens first, and a crash in between is always possible. That gap is the whole reason at-least-once produces duplicates, and it is why exactly-once business processing is not something the broker can hand you.

A tempting shortcut is to reach for the message_id property and assume RabbitMQ deduplicates on it. It does not. message_id is publisher-provided metadata that RabbitMQ carries but never inspects for deduplication. It is a fine place to put your idempotency key, but the deduplication itself is entirely your application’s responsibility. RabbitMQ recommends idempotent consumers precisely because it cannot make the guarantee for you.

So the honest interview answer is boundary-shaped, not slogan-shaped: “Confirms and acks give at-least-once on their hops. They are not atomic with the side effect, and message_id is not broker dedup, so we make the consumer idempotent.” The next section shows the pattern that does the work.

Do not overclaim

Avoid “exactly-once delivery is a myth” as a blanket line—it claims more than the sources support. State what the mechanisms do not make atomic, then show the application-level pattern that makes duplicates harmless.

04 Mechanism

Commit the dedup check in the same transaction as the work ▸

The robust pattern is derived application design, not broker magic. Give each logical operation a stable idempotency key—an order id, a request id—carried in message_id or the body. On the consumer, open one database transaction that does two things atomically: record the key in a processed-messages table protected by a unique constraint, and apply the domain write. If the key is already present, the unique constraint rejects the insert, you roll back, and the domain write never runs a second time. Only after the transaction commits do you send basic.ack.

That ordering is the point. Because the dedup record and the domain write share one commit, they cannot disagree: either both happened or neither did. Because the ack comes after the commit, a crash before the ack simply redelivers, and the redelivery hits the unique constraint and becomes a safe no-op. For operations that are naturally idempotent—setting a status to shipped, for instance—you can skip the ledger and just write the desired final state rather than applying a non-idempotent increment. The stepper animates the transaction and the redelivery that it absorbs.

06 Build

An idempotent consumer that survives a duplicate

The script publishes the same logical order twice—an at-least-once duplicate—then drains the queue through an idempotent handler. Each delivery opens one sqlite3 transaction that inserts the idempotency key into a unique-constrained table and writes the order row together; a duplicate trips the unique constraint and is caught, so the domain write runs exactly once. The asserts prove it: two deliveries, one applied, one duplicate, one row in orders.

Idempotent consumer checkpointPython 3 · Pika 1.3.2 · sqlite3

One transaction for the dedup check and the write

python · install pika==1.3.2 and run against localhost
import sqlite3
import uuid

import pika

QUEUE = f"lab.idem.{uuid.uuid4().hex[:10]}"
KEY = "order-777"

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
with db:
    db.execute("CREATE TABLE processed (idempotency_key TEXT PRIMARY KEY)")
    db.execute("CREATE TABLE orders (idempotency_key TEXT, amount INTEGER)")

connection = pika.BlockingConnection(pika.ConnectionParameters("localhost"))
channel = connection.channel()
try:
    channel.queue_declare(queue=QUEUE, durable=True, arguments={"x-queue-type": "quorum"})
    # Publish the SAME logical order twice: an at-least-once duplicate.
    for _ in range(2):
        channel.basic_publish(
            exchange="", routing_key=QUEUE, body=b"100",
            properties=pika.BasicProperties(delivery_mode=2, message_id=KEY),
        )

    applied = 0
    duplicates = 0
    while True:
        method, props, body = channel.basic_get(queue=QUEUE, auto_ack=False)
        if method is None:
            break
        key = props.message_id
        try:
            with db:  # one atomic transaction: dedup insert + domain write
                db.execute("INSERT INTO processed (idempotency_key) VALUES (?)", (key,))
                db.execute(
                    "INSERT INTO orders (idempotency_key, amount) VALUES (?, ?)",
                    (key, int(body)),
                )
            applied += 1
        except sqlite3.IntegrityError:
            duplicates += 1  # unique constraint: the domain write never ran twice
        # Ack only after the transaction resolved (commit or dedup rollback).
        channel.basic_ack(delivery_tag=method.delivery_tag)

    order_rows = db.execute("SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders").fetchone()[0]
    assert applied == 1
    assert duplicates == 1
    assert order_rows == 1  # exactly one domain write despite two deliveries
    print({"applied": applied, "duplicates": duplicates, "order_rows": order_rows})
finally:
    channel.queue_delete(queue=QUEUE)
    connection.close()
    db.close()

In production the ledger lives in the same database as the domain data so the two share a real transaction, and you prune old keys on a retention policy. RabbitMQ never sees any of this—which is the point. The broker delivers at least once; the application decides what “processed” means.

↗ Practice

Configure → Diagnose → Design

ConfigureTake a consumer that acks then writes to a database. Reorder it to write-then-ack, add a unique-constrained processed-keys table, and choose the idempotency key. State why the ack must come after the commit.
DiagnoseA colleague claims their pipeline is exactly-once because it uses confirms and manual acks. Identify the two ambiguity windows their setup still has and the false assumption about message_id.
DesignDesign idempotent handling for a non-idempotent external effect (charging a card). Decide where the idempotency key comes from, what the ledger records, and how you keep the ledger write and the charge from disagreeing.

Self-test

1. Why can’t confirms and acks give exactly-once business processing?

Because a RabbitMQ acknowledgement is not atomic with an external side effect. One of the commit and the ack always happens first, and a crash between them is possible, so an attempt can repeat. The mechanisms give at-least-once on their hops, not exactly-once across a database or API.

2. What is the difference between at-most-once and at-least-once?

At-most-once handles a message zero or one times: no duplicates, but possible loss. At-least-once handles it one or more times: no silent loss, but possible duplicates. You choose per hop based on which failure is cheaper.

3. Where do the two duplicate windows occur?

On producer→RabbitMQ when a confirm is lost after acceptance and the producer republishes, and on RabbitMQ→consumer when the ack is lost after the database commit and RabbitMQ redelivers.

4. Does RabbitMQ deduplicate on message_id?

No. message_id is publisher-provided metadata the broker carries but never inspects for deduplication. It is a good place to store an idempotency key, but the dedup logic is the application’s.

5. Why must the dedup record and the domain write share one transaction?

So they cannot disagree: either both commit or neither does. If they were separate, a crash could record the key without doing the work, or do the work without recording the key, breaking idempotency.

6. Is a durable queue enough to guarantee at-least-once across a restart?

No. The message must also be published persistent, and confirms tell the publisher when that durability point is reached. Quorum queues bind confirms to quorum acceptance, but only while the majority holds; an unconfirmed message has no such guarantee.
