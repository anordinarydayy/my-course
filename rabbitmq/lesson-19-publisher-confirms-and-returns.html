Publisher Confirms & Returns

A plain publish returns nothing. The producer needs a signal that RabbitMQ accepted the message, a separate signal that a mandatory publish found no queue, and the discipline to track both without assuming they arrive in order. This lesson builds that machinery and states exactly what a positive confirm does and does not prove.

00 Failure first

A plain publish tells the producer nothing

By default, basic.publish is fire-and-forget. The client writes bytes to a channel and moves on. If the broker was overloaded and dropped the connection, if the exchange existed but no queue matched, or if a routed queue rejected the message under an overflow policy, the producer never finds out. “It didn’t throw” is not evidence of anything; it only means the local socket write did not fail.

Publisher confirms fix the producer half of the reliability problem. They are the mirror image of consumer acknowledgements from Lesson 18: an ack covers RabbitMQ→consumer, a confirm covers producer→RabbitMQ. When confirms are on, the broker promises to send back exactly one verdict for every message the channel publishes—a positive basic.ack or a negative basic.nack—so the producer can retry, alert, or record success on real evidence.

But a confirm answers a narrower question than most people assume, and it interacts with a second, independent signal: the basic.return that a mandatory publish gets when it cannot be routed. The rest of this lesson pins down precisely what each one means, why they can both fire for the same message, and how to track them without corrupting your own bookkeeping.

Two producer-side signals

Confirm: did RabbitMQ accept responsibility for this publish? Return: did a mandatory publish fail to route to any queue? They are different questions with different answers, and you often need both.

01 Mechanism

confirm.select, sequence numbers, and one verdict per publish ▸

Confirms are enabled per channel by sending confirm.select once. From then on, RabbitMQ assigns every published message a monotonically increasing publish sequence number starting at 1, and it will return one confirmation for each—possibly grouped as a range with the multiple flag. A channel cannot be both transactional and in confirm mode: the two are mutually exclusive, so you choose one publisher-safety mechanism per channel.

A positive confirm means RabbitMQ reached its queue-specific responsibility boundary for that message, which depends on where the message went. For an unroutable publish, the broker confirms once it determines there is no target queue. For a routable publish, it confirms once every routed queue has accepted the message. A persistent message routed to a durable queue is confirmed after it is persisted to disk; a message routed to a quorum queue is confirmed after a quorum of replicas accepts it. That is the whole promise—it must not be shortened to “the broker stored it safely forever.”

The step-through animates a routable publish confirmed after the queue accepts it, then an unroutable mandatory publish where the basic.return arrives before the confirm. Watch the outstanding map: an entry is added on publish and removed only when its verdict lands.

02 Boundary

An unroutable message can be positively confirmed

The most surprising and most important fact about confirms: an unroutable publish can receive a positive basic.ack. Confirms report broker handling, not successful routing. If you publish to an existing exchange with a key no queue is bound to, the broker does its job—it evaluates the bindings, finds nothing, and confirms that it handled the message—then, unless you asked otherwise, the message is discarded. A confirm alone therefore cannot tell “accepted and routed to a destination queue” from “accepted but routed nowhere.”

That gap is what mandatory=true closes. When a mandatory publish cannot be routed to any queue, RabbitMQ sends the message back to the publisher with basic.return, and it does so before that publish’s basic.ack or basic.nack. So a producer that must distinguish the two outcomes needs both mechanisms wired up: return handling to catch the unroutable case, and confirm tracking to catch acceptance and negative acknowledgements. Neither is redundant.

03 Bookkeeping

Confirms are asynchronous, grouped, and not in publish order

The efficient way to use confirms is to keep publishing and reconcile verdicts as they arrive. That means holding an outstanding map keyed by publish sequence number: add an entry when you publish, remove it when a positive confirm covers it, and act when a negative confirm or a timeout covers it. Two properties of the confirm stream make naive code wrong. First, confirms are asynchronous—the broker sends them when its work completes, not synchronously with your call. Second, a single confirm can cover a range with multiple=true (“everything up to sequence N”), and confirms need not arrive in publish order, because different destination queues finish at different times.

So “publish one message, wait for its confirm, repeat” is both slow and conceptually misleading: it is documented as a throughput anti-pattern, and it hides the range and ordering behavior you must handle at scale. The robust shapes are streaming confirms with an asynchronous callback that clears ranges from the map, or bounded batches where you publish a window and then wait for the whole window to clear before opening the next.

A negative confirm (basic.nack) is rare and means RabbitMQ could not take responsibility—an internal error, or a routed queue rejecting under an overflow policy. It is not the same as a return: a return says “no queue matched,” while a nack says “I could not accept this.” Your bookkeeping treats them differently, and the next section shows how a nack can even coexist with partial success.

Bookkeeping trap

Removing the wrong entries on a multiple confirm is a classic bug: a multiple=true ack for sequence N clears every unconfirmed entry with a sequence ≤ N, not just N. Treat the confirm as a high-water mark, not a single key.

04 Trade-off

The lost confirm and the partial fanout both create duplicates ▸

Confirms buy at-least-once publishing, and at-least-once always admits duplicates. Two windows produce them. The first is the lost confirm: RabbitMQ accepts and persists a message, sends the confirm, and the connection drops before it arrives. The producer is now in an uncertainty window—the message may have been accepted or may not have—and the only safe choice for at-least-once is to republish every unconfirmed message on reconnect, which can duplicate a message the broker already has. The producer must retain unconfirmed messages; the consumer must tolerate duplicates.

The second window is the partial multi-queue route. A single publish can fan out to several queues. If one destination queue is full under a reject-publish overflow policy, RabbitMQ can nack the whole publish even though other destination queues already accepted the message. Republishing the entire message then duplicates it in the queues that never rejected it. The stepper walks both windows so the duplicate source is unmistakable.

05 Comparison

Transactions are not “the same guarantee, only slower”

AMQP 0-9-1 also offers transactions (tx.select / tx.commit), and it is tempting to describe them as confirms with a performance penalty. That is wrong on both counts. RabbitMQ documents transactions as heavyweight and mutually exclusive with confirms, and neither one is an end-to-end, consumer-processing transaction—both are publisher-side safety mechanisms with different shapes. Confirms are asynchronous and pipelined; a transaction batches publishes and blocks on commit. There is no universal “transactions are N times slower” law to quote as production truth; the right framing is that confirms are the recommended high-throughput mechanism and transactions are a heavier, synchronous alternative.

The client library also shapes how you experience confirms and returns. In Pika 1.3.2’s blocking adapter you enable confirms with BlockingChannel.confirm_delivery(). After that, a mandatory publish that the broker returns raises pika.exceptions.UnroutableError, and a broker nack raises pika.exceptions.NackError. Those two exceptions map to the two negative outcomes we have been separating—unroutable versus not-accepted—so the blocking API forces you to handle them distinctly. This is Pika’s design, not an AMQP-wide contract; an asynchronous client exposes the same information through callbacks instead.

Interview follow-up

“Confirms or transactions?” Answer with the boundary, not a slogan: “Confirms, because they pipeline and are the recommended mechanism. Transactions are supported but heavier and synchronous, and neither makes publish and downstream consumption one atomic operation.”

06 Build

A publisher that separates confirmed, returned, and nacked

The script enables confirms on the channel, then publishes two mandatory messages: one to a bound routing key and one to a key with no queue. The routable publish is confirmed and lands in the queue; the unroutable one raises UnroutableError, caught separately from NackError. The asserts prove the distinction the lesson turns on—an unroutable, mandatory publish is returned, not silently accepted into a queue—and the final depth shows only the routed message arrived.

Publisher confirms checkpointPython 3 · Pika 1.3.2

Confirm, return, and nack are three different outcomes

python · install pika==1.3.2 and run against localhost
import uuid

import pika
from pika.exceptions import NackError, UnroutableError

EXCHANGE = f"lab.confirms.direct.{uuid.uuid4().hex[:8]}"
QUEUE = f"lab.confirms.q.{uuid.uuid4().hex[:8]}"
KEY_OK = "route.ok"
KEY_MISSING = "route.missing"

connection = pika.BlockingConnection(pika.ConnectionParameters("localhost"))
channel = connection.channel()
channel.confirm_delivery()  # channel-scoped: each publish now returns a verdict
try:
    channel.exchange_declare(exchange=EXCHANGE, exchange_type="direct", durable=True)
    channel.queue_declare(queue=QUEUE, durable=True, arguments={"x-queue-type": "quorum"})
    channel.queue_bind(queue=QUEUE, exchange=EXCHANGE, routing_key=KEY_OK)

    outcomes = {}

    # Routable + mandatory: confirmed after the routed queue accepts it.
    channel.basic_publish(
        exchange=EXCHANGE, routing_key=KEY_OK, body=b"order-1",
        properties=pika.BasicProperties(delivery_mode=2), mandatory=True,
    )
    outcomes["routable"] = "confirmed"

    # Unroutable + mandatory: the broker returns it before the confirm.
    try:
        channel.basic_publish(
            exchange=EXCHANGE, routing_key=KEY_MISSING, body=b"order-2",
            properties=pika.BasicProperties(delivery_mode=2), mandatory=True,
        )
        outcomes["unroutable"] = "unexpected-accept-into-queue"
    except UnroutableError:
        outcomes["unroutable"] = "returned"
    except NackError:
        outcomes["unroutable"] = "nacked"

    depth = channel.queue_declare(queue=QUEUE, passive=True).method.message_count
    assert outcomes["routable"] == "confirmed"
    assert outcomes["unroutable"] == "returned"  # returned, not nacked, not lost
    assert depth == 1                            # only the routed message landed
    print({"outcomes": outcomes, "queue_depth": depth})
finally:
    channel.queue_delete(queue=QUEUE)
    channel.exchange_delete(exchange=EXCHANGE)
    connection.close()

Publishing to a nonexistent exchange is a different failure entirely: it is a channel error that closes the channel, not an unroutable return. Keep that case out of your return handler—it is a topology bug, not a routing miss.

Self-test

1. What does a positive publisher confirm actually prove?

That RabbitMQ reached its acceptance boundary for the destination: no-queue decision for an unroutable publish, acceptance by every routed queue for a routable one, persistence for a persistent message to a durable queue, and quorum acceptance for a quorum queue. It does not prove consumption.

2. Can an unroutable message receive a positive confirm?

Yes. Confirms report broker handling, not routing. To detect the unroutable case you also need mandatory=true and a basic.return handler.

3. In what order do the return and the confirm arrive for a returned mandatory publish?

The basic.return arrives first, before that publish’s basic.ack or basic.nack. A producer that only watches confirms will miss the return.

4. Why is “publish, wait for confirm, repeat” discouraged?

It is a throughput anti-pattern and it hides real behavior: confirms are asynchronous, can cover ranges via multiple, and need not arrive in publish order. Stream confirms or use bounded batches instead.

5. How can a fanout publish be nacked yet still have partly succeeded?

If one destination queue rejects under reject-publish while others accept, RabbitMQ can nack the whole publish. Republishing the entire message duplicates it in the queues that already accepted it.

6. How do confirms compare to AMQP transactions?

Both are publisher-side safety mechanisms and are mutually exclusive on a channel. Transactions are heavier and synchronous; confirms are asynchronous and recommended for throughput. Neither makes publish and downstream consumption atomic, and there is no fixed slowdown multiplier to quote.
