Consumer Acknowledgements & Redelivery

An acknowledgement is a promise about exactly one hop: RabbitMQ delivered a message and the consumer accepts responsibility for it. Get the timing wrong and you either lose work or drown in duplicates. This lesson separates automatic from manual mode, shows how unacked deliveries are requeued, and treats the redelivery flag as the hint it actually is.

Original study chapter. The scenarios, diagrams, code, and SVGs are written from scratch. Stable behavior targets open-source RabbitMQ 4.3 (latest signed patch overlay 4.3.3); runnable code uses Python 3 and Pika 1.3.2. This chapter contains three original figures, two interactive. Acknowledgement mechanics are protocol behavior, not a substitute for application-level idempotency.
00 Failure first

Automatic acknowledgement quietly drops the message a crash interrupts

A consumer acknowledgement answers one narrow question: has RabbitMQ’s delivery of a message to a consumer been accounted for? It says nothing about whether a producer’s publish reached the broker—that is a publisher confirm, a separate mechanism on a different hop—and it is not an application-to-application receipt. The two are orthogonal: neither observes the other, and confusing them is the first reliability bug most teams ship.

Automatic acknowledgement (often called autoAck, or no_ack in older AMQP 0-9-1 phrasing) tells RabbitMQ to treat a delivery as successful the instant it writes the message to the connection. There is no later step where the consumer confirms it finished. If the process crashes, the socket buffer is discarded, or the handler raises after the bytes have left the broker, that message is simply gone. No redelivery, no error, no trace in the queue.

That is not a defect; it is the contract. Automatic acknowledgement trades safety for throughput and simplicity, and it removes the prefetch back-pressure window that only manual mode has, so a fast broker can bury a slow consumer in memory. For best-effort telemetry, cache warm-ups, or a live metrics feed where one dropped sample is irrelevant, that trade is defensible. For an order, a payment, or anything whose processing must not silently vanish, it is the wrong default. Everything that follows is about the manual alternative and the redelivery behavior that makes it safe.

Orthogonality invariant

A consumer ack covers RabbitMQ→consumer. A publisher confirm covers producer→RabbitMQ. Neither one proves the other happened, and neither proves an external side effect committed. Reason about one hop at a time.

01 Mechanism

Manual acknowledgement transfers responsibility only when you send it ▸

In manual mode a delivery stays the broker’s responsibility until the consumer sends a positive basic.ack. Only then may RabbitMQ mark the message for deletion. The discipline is therefore simple to state and easy to get wrong: acknowledge after the work whose durability matters has completed, such as committing the database row—never before. The unacknowledged message is your retry token; give it up only once the outcome is safe.

Each delivery carries a delivery-tag, a monotonically increasing integer that is scoped to the channel that received it. You must acknowledge it on that same channel. An unknown tag, a tag already acknowledged, or a tag from another channel is a protocol violation that closes the channel with an error—so a common bug is handing work to a thread pool and acknowledging from the wrong channel. Setting multiple=true acknowledges every outstanding delivery up to and including the supplied tag, which lets a consumer batch acknowledgements and cut round trips.

The step-through below follows one delivery through the happy path and then through a lost connection. Watch where responsibility lives at each moment: it never leaves the broker until the basic.ack arrives.

02 Invariant

An unacked delivery is outstanding on its channel, not lost

The core guarantee of manual mode is recovery on failure. If the channel or the whole connection closes—whether from a detected TCP loss, a client bug, or the consumer process dying—RabbitMQ automatically requeues every delivery that was outstanding on that channel and had not been acknowledged. Those messages are not discarded and they are not frozen; they become ready again for another consumer. This is why manual acknowledgement can promise at-least-once processing attempts for the hop.

It is tempting to describe an unacked message as “waiting for the consumer to die.” That is misleading. The delivery is simply outstanding: it remains assigned to that channel until one of a small set of events occurs—an ack, a nack or reject, the channel or connection closing, or, on a queue type that supports it, a delivery-acknowledgement timeout. In RabbitMQ 4.3 that timeout is a quorum-queue feature, not a universal deadline on every queue, so do not build application logic around a fixed cancellation time; the stable, portable rule is only that a timed-out or connection-lost unacked delivery becomes eligible for redelivery.

When RabbitMQ redelivers a message it knows it previously delivered and requeued, it sets the redelivered flag to true. Read that flag precisely. true means “this consumer may have seen this message before”—not “the previous attempt committed its work.” The previous consumer might have crashed before doing anything, halfway through, or after committing but before acking. false means RabbitMQ has no record of a prior delivery of that message. The flag is a diagnostic hint, never a deduplication key, and never a substitute for idempotency at the side-effect boundary.

Do not overclaim

redelivered=true does not prove prior processing, and it is not unique per logical message—two different deliveries of two different messages can both carry it. If you need to recognise a repeat, you need your own stable idempotency key, which Lesson 20 develops in full.

03 Boundary

reject is one delivery; nack adds bulk — and 4.3 counts them differently ▸

Positive acknowledgement is only half the vocabulary. A consumer that cannot process a delivery has two negative signals. basic.reject acts on a single delivery. basic.nack is the RabbitMQ extension that adds a multiple flag, so it can reject every unacknowledged delivery up to a tag in one call. Both take a requeue argument: requeue=true returns the delivery for another attempt, while requeue=false discards it—or dead-letters it when the queue has a dead-letter exchange configured, which Lesson 21 builds on.

RabbitMQ 4.3 adds a distinction that matters for poison handling on quorum queues. A quorum queue tracks two counters. acquired-count rises every time a message is handed to a consumer. delivery-count is the failure-oriented counter that a delivery limit is measured against. In 4.3, a basic.reject is treated as a failed attempt and advances delivery-count; an explicit basic.nack return is treated as a deliberate return and advances acquired-count only. The practical consequence is sharp: a basic.nack(requeue=true) loop is not guaranteed to reach the quorum delivery limit, because it never moves the counter the limit watches.

This is a 4.3-specific behavior. Do not generalise it to RabbitMQ 4.2, where every requeue counted toward the limit, and do not attribute it to classic queues, which have no built-in delivery-limit machinery at all. The stepper contrasts the dispositions and shows the counters diverging.

04 Trade-off

Immediate requeue can become a hot loop; bound it

Requeue is convenient and dangerous. RabbitMQ tries to restore a requeued message near its original position, but concurrent deliveries and acknowledgements mean the position is best effort, not exact FIFO restoration—the message can land nearer the head and be redelivered immediately. If the failure is transient and every consumer keeps rejecting-and-requeueing the same message, the result is a CPU- and network-intensive hot loop: the broker redelivers, the consumer fails, requeues, and the cycle repeats thousands of times a second while doing no useful work.

The fix is to bound retries rather than rely on an unbounded immediate requeue. On a 4.3 quorum queue you can use native delayed retry, which applies a bounded linear backoff based on the delivery count instead of redelivering instantly. Alternatively, route exhausted or non-retryable messages to a parking path with requeue=false plus a dead-letter exchange. What you must not do is assume the quorum delivery limit will save you from a nack(requeue=true) loop—as the previous section showed, that specific loop never advances the counter the limit watches.

Step back to the top-level trade. Automatic acknowledgement gives at-most-once processing for the hop: no broker-driven duplicates, but silent loss on failure. Manual acknowledgement with requeue gives at-least-once: no silent loss, but a duplicate is possible whenever a consumer does its work and then fails before the ack lands. Neither is “correct” in the abstract; you choose per queue based on whether loss or duplication is the cheaper failure.

05 Diagnosis

Read the duplicate window in a real consumer

Put the pieces on a timeline for one order. RabbitMQ delivers it; the consumer opens a transaction, writes the order row, and commits; then it sends basic.ack. Between the commit and the ack there is a small but real window. If the process dies there, the database change is durable but the ack never reached the broker, so RabbitMQ requeues the delivery and a second consumer receives it with redelivered=true. Without protection, that consumer writes the order a second time. The window is inherent to at-least-once: you cannot make the commit and the ack a single atomic action across two systems.

You close the window at the application boundary, not in the broker. Give each logical operation a stable idempotency key—an order id, a request id—and make the side effect recognise a repeat: a unique constraint, an upsert, or a processed-message record checked in the same transaction as the domain write. Then a redelivery re-runs the handler harmlessly. The publisher-supplied message_id property can carry that key, but note that RabbitMQ never inspects it for you—deduplication is entirely your design.

The same reasoning drives everyday diagnosis. A steadily climbing unacked count usually means consumers are slow, blocked, or forgetting to ack—not that messages are lost. A single message redelivered thousands of times is a poison message or an unbounded requeue loop, and on a quorum queue you check whether the loop is using nack(requeue=true), which will not trip the delivery limit. Batching with multiple=true trades round trips for a wider loss window: if the channel drops before a batched ack, every delivery in that batch is redelivered. Choose the batch size against how expensive re-processing is.

Interview follow-up

“Where is the duplicate?” The honest answer names the hop: “Between committing the side effect and sending the ack. At-least-once guarantees the attempt is retried, not that it happens once, so the consumer is idempotent on a stable key. Acks and confirms do not make the commit and the ack atomic.”

06 Build

A manual-ack consumer that survives a redelivery

The script below proves the invariant end to end against a real broker. It publishes one order, deliberately requeues it once with basic.nack(requeue=true) to simulate a transient failure, then processes the redelivered copy through an idempotent handler keyed by message_id. The asserts encode the lesson: exactly one redelivery is observed, the side effect runs once, and the queue drains. The retry is bounded to a single attempt so it can never become a hot loop, and the lab queue is deleted in finally.

Manual acknowledgement checkpointPython 3 · Pika 1.3.2

Ack after the work, tolerate the redelivery

python · install pika==1.3.2 and run against localhost
import uuid

import pika

QUEUE = f"lab.ack.redelivery.{uuid.uuid4().hex[:10]}"

connection = pika.BlockingConnection(pika.ConnectionParameters("localhost"))
channel = connection.channel()
try:
    channel.queue_declare(
        queue=QUEUE,
        durable=True,
        arguments={"x-queue-type": "quorum"},
    )
    channel.basic_publish(
        exchange="",
        routing_key=QUEUE,
        body=b"order-1001",
        properties=pika.BasicProperties(delivery_mode=2, message_id="order-1001"),
    )

    processed = set()
    attempts = {}
    redelivered_seen = 0

    def handle(message_id):
        # Idempotent side effect keyed by a stable id: safe under redelivery.
        processed.add(message_id)

    while True:
        method, props, body = channel.basic_get(queue=QUEUE, auto_ack=False)
        if method is None:
            break
        if method.redelivered:
            redelivered_seen += 1
        key = props.message_id
        attempts[key] = attempts.get(key, 0) + 1
        # Bounded transient retry: requeue exactly once, then stop looping.
        if attempts[key] == 1:
            channel.basic_nack(delivery_tag=method.delivery_tag, requeue=True)
            continue
        handle(key)                                          # durable work first
        channel.basic_ack(delivery_tag=method.delivery_tag)  # then ack, same channel

    assert redelivered_seen == 1
    assert processed == {"order-1001"}
    depth = channel.queue_declare(queue=QUEUE, passive=True).method.message_count
    assert depth == 0
    print(
        {
            "processed": sorted(processed),
            "redelivered_seen": redelivered_seen,
            "queue_depth": depth,
        }
    )
finally:
    channel.queue_delete(queue=QUEUE)
    connection.close()

Swap the single-shot bound for a real policy in production: native quorum delayed retry, or a dead-letter path for exhausted attempts. The shape stays the same—work first, ack second, and a handler that treats a redelivery as a no-op rather than a second charge.

Configure → Diagnose → Design

ConfigureConvert an autoAck consumer to manual mode. Choose a prefetch, decide the exact point to call basic.ack, and pick reject versus nack(requeue=false) for a validation failure. State which channel the ack must travel on and why a thread pool can break that.
DiagnoseA quorum queue’s unacked count is flat but one message has been redelivered tens of thousands of times. Explain why a basic.nack(requeue=true) loop need not reach the 4.3 delivery limit, and name two changes that bound it.
DesignA consumer’s side effect is a non-idempotent external charge. Identify the duplicate window precisely, choose a stable idempotency key, and describe how a processed-message record in the same transaction makes redelivery safe.

Self-test

1. How does a consumer acknowledgement differ from a publisher confirm?

They cover different hops. An ack covers RabbitMQ→consumer; a confirm covers producer→RabbitMQ. They are orthogonal—neither observes the other, and neither is an application-to-application receipt.

2. When exactly should a consumer send basic.ack?

After the work whose durability matters has completed—typically after the database transaction commits. The unacknowledged message is the retry token; releasing it earlier risks silent loss on failure.

3. What happens to an unacked manual delivery when its channel or connection closes?

RabbitMQ automatically requeues it, making it ready for another consumer. On redelivery the redelivered flag is set. The message was outstanding, not lost.

4. Does redelivered=true prove the previous consumer processed the message?

No. It means RabbitMQ delivered and requeued the message before; the prior attempt may have done nothing, part of the work, or committed before failing to ack. Prior processing is unknown.

5. In RabbitMQ 4.3, why might a basic.nack(requeue=true) loop never hit a quorum queue’s delivery limit?

An explicit nack return advances acquired-count but not delivery-count, and the delivery limit is measured against delivery-count. Only a basic.reject advances it. Bound the loop with delayed retry or a terminal policy.

6. When is automatic acknowledgement a defensible choice?

For best-effort or telemetry workloads where losing a message on failure is acceptable, and where you accept giving up the prefetch back-pressure window that only manual mode provides.
