Queue Length Limits & Overflow

A queue limit does not create consumer capacity. It names the ready state to count and decides who loses when producers outrun consumers: old work, new work, or an upstream request. Correct overload design begins with that business choice, then adds confirms, retries, and physical storage headroom.

Original study chapter. The overload traces, code, decision table, and SVGs are original. Stable behavior targets open-source RabbitMQ 4.3; runnable code uses Pika 1.3.2. This chapter contains three original figures, two interactive. Logical byte limits are not disk quotas.
00 Failure first

A full queue cannot preserve every promise

Publishers sustain 2,000 jobs per second while consumers acknowledge 1,500. The backlog grows by 500 per second. Adding max-length=10000 stops unbounded ready count, but it cannot make the missing 500 jobs per second disappear. At the threshold, RabbitMQ must remove old ready work or reject incoming work.

That choice encodes a business invariant. A telemetry dashboard may value fresh readings and accept losing old ones. An order queue may forbid dropping accepted work and instead require producer-visible rejection, upstream backoff, and idempotent retry. A limit is therefore an overload policy, not a generic safety checkbox.

Overflow invariant

Length and byte limits constrain ready queue state. They do not count unacknowledged deliveries, do not cap physical disk usage, and do not supply missing processing capacity. Overflow determines which party absorbs pressure.

01 Accounting

Count and bytes apply to ready messages—not unacknowledged work ▸

max-length in policy, or x-max-length at declaration, caps the number of ready messages. max-length-bytes / x-max-length-bytes sums ready message body lengths. Properties, headers, queue indexes, logs, replication copies, and implementation overhead are excluded. If both limits exist, the first one reached is enforced.

Once a message is delivered but not acknowledged, it leaves the ready accounting set. That does not free its body from broker memory or disk, and it does not mean processing completed. A queue can show two ready messages at a length limit plus many unacknowledged deliveries outside that limit.

Production warning

max-length-bytes is a logical body-byte guardrail, not a disk quota. Separately model and monitor properties, unacked messages, logs, indexes, replication, dead-letter state, segment/compaction headroom, and free space.

02 Default overflow

drop-head makes room for the newest ready message ▸

Once a limit exists, the default overflow mode is drop-head. When a new publication would exceed the limit, RabbitMQ removes the oldest ready message and admits the new one. If a DLX is configured, the removed head can be dead-lettered; otherwise it is discarded.

The mechanism favors freshness. That is sensible for replaceable sensor snapshots and dangerous for orders: a message that was accepted earlier can vanish because newer work arrived. A publisher confirm for the new message does not notify the producer that an older accepted message was sacrificed.

03 Producer-visible pressure

Rejection becomes useful only when publishers observe confirms

reject-publish preserves the existing ready backlog and discards the incoming publication. With publisher confirms enabled, RabbitMQ sends a negative acknowledgement. The producer can then back off, reject its upstream request, or retry through an idempotent path. Without confirm handling, the intended backpressure signal never becomes an application decision.

reject-publish-dlx also dead-letters the rejected incoming message when valid DLX topology exists. This moves pressure to another destination; it does not eliminate it. Size, monitor, and define ownership for the dead-letter path.

Routing creates an important non-atomic case. If one publication targets several queues and any target rejects it, the publisher receives a nack even though other queues may already have accepted their copies. A nack is not rollback across a route graph. Retrying can duplicate accepted copies, so downstream idempotency and route-aware reconciliation remain necessary.

Interview follow-up

If one of three routed queues rejects a publication, did none receive it? No. The producer gets a nack, but other destinations may have accepted their copies. RabbitMQ does not turn multi-queue routing into an atomic transaction.

04 Queue-type boundaries

Quorum limits are approximate at rejection; streams use retention

Quorum queues support drop-head and reject-publish, but not reject-publish-dlx. With rejection, in-flight publications mean a quorum queue can overshoot the configured limit by a small workload-dependent amount. Do not document a quorum max length as an exact hard cap.

Streams do not implement traditional ready-length overflow. Their max-length-bytes and max-age settings remove old log segments. At least one segment containing messages remains, and segment granularity can leave retention above the requested threshold. Stream retention policy values take precedence over declaration arguments.

05 Trade-offs

Choose who absorbs pressure, then make the loss visible

Prefer policies for mutable max length, byte limit, and overflow when the queue type supports them. A policy can change without redeploying every declarer. Still benchmark the transition: a policy update can take time on a busy topology, and changing overflow alters the business victim immediately.

06 Worked scenario

Prove rejection, then prove unacknowledged work is outside the limit

The lab creates a classic queue with ready count two and reject-publish. Confirmed publishes A and B fill it. C is nacked. Fetching A without acknowledging moves it out of ready accounting, so a new C publish is accepted even while A remains broker-held and unacknowledged.

Complete ready-count proofPython 3 · Pika 1.3.2

Use confirms to turn overflow into an application signal

python · install pika==1.3.2 and run against localhost
import pika

connection = pika.BlockingConnection(pika.ConnectionParameters("localhost"))
channel = connection.channel()
try:
    queue = channel.queue_declare(
        queue="",
        exclusive=True,
        arguments={
            "x-queue-type": "classic",
            "x-max-length": 2,
            "x-overflow": "reject-publish",
        },
    ).method.queue
    channel.confirm_delivery()

    channel.basic_publish("", queue, b"A", mandatory=True)
    channel.basic_publish("", queue, b"B", mandatory=True)

    rejected = False
    try:
        channel.basic_publish("", queue, b"C-rejected", mandatory=True)
    except pika.exceptions.NackError:
        rejected = True
    assert rejected is True

    method, _properties, body = channel.basic_get(queue, auto_ack=False)
    assert body == b"A"  # A is now unacknowledged, not ready.
    channel.basic_publish("", queue, b"C-accepted", mandatory=True)
    channel.basic_ack(method.delivery_tag)

    remaining = []
    while True:
        method, _properties, body = channel.basic_get(queue, auto_ack=True)
        if method is None:
            break
        remaining.append(body.decode())
    assert remaining == ["B", "C-accepted"]
    print({"third_publish_nacked": rejected, "remaining_ready": remaining})
finally:
    connection.close()

The first C never entered the queue because the ready set was full. After A became unacknowledged, the ready count fell to one and the next C fit. This is exactly why a ready byte/count limit cannot be used as a total memory, disk, or in-flight-work quota.

↗ Practice

Configure → Diagnose → Design

ConfigureA queue has max-length=1000 and max-length-bytes=10 MB. Calculate which limit fires for 2 KB, 20 KB, and mixed bodies, remembering that properties and unacked messages are excluded.
DiagnoseA queue remains exactly at its limit while producers report no errors and old orders disappear. Identify the likely mode, explain why confirms for new messages can still succeed, and specify the metrics/logs that expose loss.
DesignOne event routes to billing, analytics, and archive; billing uses reject-publish while the others accept. Design producer retry and consumer deduplication around the non-atomic nack, and decide whether the route graph should be split.

Self-test

1. Which messages count toward max-length?

Ready messages only. Delivered but unacknowledged messages do not count toward the ready length limit.

2. What does max-length-bytes include?

Ready message body lengths. It excludes properties, implementation overhead, unacked work, replication, logs, and physical storage headroom.

3. What happens under the default drop-head overflow mode?

RabbitMQ removes the oldest ready message, dead-lettering it only when configured, and admits the incoming publication.

4. How does reject-publish reach the producer?

With publisher confirms enabled, RabbitMQ negatively acknowledges the rejected incoming publish; the application must handle that outcome.

5. Why is a multi-route nack not an atomic rollback?

One target may reject and cause the nack while other target queues have already accepted their independent copies.

6. Which overflow mode is unsupported by quorum queues?

reject-publish-dlx. Quorum supports drop-head and reject-publish, with possible small rejection overshoot.
