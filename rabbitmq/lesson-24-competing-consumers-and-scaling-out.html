24Consumers & throughput

Competing Consumers & Scaling Out

Attaching several consumers to one queue is RabbitMQ’s built-in horizontal scaling for work. It is powerful and it is bounded: round-robin is delivery distribution, not equal effort; adding workers helps only until some resource binds; and FIFO on the wire is not ordered side effects in your application.

Original study chapter. The scenario, worker model, figures, code, and SVGs are original. Stable behavior targets open-source RabbitMQ 4.3 (latest signed patch overlay 4.3.3; public release and docs pages still showed 4.3.2 when researched); runnable code uses Python 3 and Pika 1.3.2. This chapter contains three original figures, two interactive. Throughput figures are teaching estimates, never RabbitMQ guarantees.
00 Failure first

The team that doubled its workers and saw nothing improve

A service is falling behind on a single busy queue, so the team scales the consumer deployment from four workers to eight, then to sixteen. Throughput barely moves. Worse, a downstream report shows events applied out of the order they were published, and one worker is somehow handling twice as many messages as another. Every instinct — “more consumers must mean more throughput,” “round-robin must mean equal work,” “FIFO in must mean FIFO out” — turns out to be an approximation that breaks under load.

None of this is misconfiguration. It is what competing consumers actually guarantee, which is narrower than the folklore. RabbitMQ deals ready messages across the consumers that can currently accept them; it does not promise equal effort, equal counts, ordered completion, or unlimited speedup. This lesson replaces each slogan with the precise rule, so you can predict where scaling out works and where it quietly stops.

Competing-consumers invariant

Ready messages are distributed round-robin among the active, eligible consumers of the relevant highest priority. Distribution is about who receives a delivery next — not about how long each takes, how many each ends up with, or the order in which side effects land.

01 Mechanism

Round-robin selects the next consumer that can accept work

With no single-active-consumer setting and no unequal priorities, RabbitMQ selects among the queue’s consumers in round-robin order. But selection has an eligibility filter. A consumer whose prefetch window is full has no credit, so it is skipped. A consumer whose network connection cannot currently accept a write is skipped. Selection rotates to the next consumer that can actually take a delivery, rather than waiting for a blocked one.

When consumer priorities are in play, the rule is layered: the highest-priority consumers that are currently active receive work, and consumers tied at that highest active priority round-robin among themselves. Lower-priority consumers get deliveries only when every higher-priority consumer is blocked. So “round-robin” always means “round-robin among the eligible consumers of the relevant highest priority,” not “round-robin among everyone attached.”

This is why prefetch and distribution interact. A larger window lets a fast worker keep pulling, which can be exactly what you want; a smaller window returns undelivered work to the pool sooner so a free worker can take it. The distribution rule is fixed, but the window size you choose changes how evenly work spreads when consumers process at different speeds.

02 Invariant

Distribution is not equal work — watch a skip happen ▸

Step through three equal-priority consumers sharing a queue with a window of one each. Watch delivery rotate, watch a blocked consumer get skipped rather than waited for, and watch the delivered counts come out uneven even though the rule is “round-robin.” Equal distribution of turns is not equal distribution of completed work when processing times differ.

The lesson of the skip is that RabbitMQ never holds a delivery for a consumer that cannot take it. That protects overall throughput, but it means the count each worker handles depends on how fast each acknowledges, not on a promise of parity. If you need even counts, that is an application concern, not something round-robin provides.

03 Boundary

Adding consumers is a lever with a hard stop

More consumers raise egress only while the queue still has capacity to hand out and the consumers are genuinely the bottleneck. Because a single queue replica has a single-core hot path for its own work, one queue’s delivery rate eventually plateaus no matter how many consumers you attach. Before that, other limits often bind first: client CPU, a slow downstream dependency, the network, storage throughput, acknowledgement overhead, or quorum replication cost. Past the binding resource, each new worker adds coordination without adding output.

The fix is not more workers on one queue; it is more queues. Partition the workload across several queues — by a stable key so related work stays together — and give each its own consumers, or use partitioned streams (super streams) when the semantics fit. That spreads the hot path across replicas and cores instead of contending on one.

Prefetch shapes this too, but avoid the absolute claim that an unlimited window always dumps the whole queue on one consumer. What a high or unlimited per-consumer window actually does is let a worker accumulate a large private in-flight backlog, especially when workers join at different times or process at different speeds. The realised distribution still depends on eligibility, timing, priorities, and queue type. Lower the window to return work to the pool sooner; raise it to keep a fast worker fed — and measure which your workload needs.

Scaling trap

“Throughput is flat, add more consumers.” If the queue’s single-core hot path, a downstream service, or replication is the limit, more consumers on the same queue cannot help. Shard into multiple queues or streams instead, and confirm the real bottleneck with metrics before scaling.

04 Ordering

FIFO enqueue and dequeue is not ordered side effects ▸

Messages published on one channel are enqueued in publish order, and a queue hands them out in that order. But two things break the illusion of end-to-end ordering. First, concurrent publisher channels or connections interleave, so “publish order” across producers is already not well defined. Second — and this is the one that surprises teams — multiple consumers can receive messages in FIFO order yet finish, acknowledge, fail, or requeue them in a different order. Redelivery and priorities perturb delivery order too.

So ordering needs an explicit topology and an execution invariant. The queue-oriented option for one strictly sequential stream is a single active consumer (or exactly one consumer) plus an application that does not process callbacks concurrently and handles redeliveries idempotently. To keep parallelism while preserving per-entity order, partition by a stable key into several queues — each with a single active consumer — or use streams and super streams where offsets preserve log order within a partition. The next lesson develops single active consumer in depth.

05 Decide

Match the lever to the bottleneck

Scaling decisions go wrong when the lever does not match the limit. If the queue’s own hot path is saturated, sharding helps and more consumers do not. If a downstream API is the wall, neither helps until the dependency is fixed. The map below pairs each observed limit with why more workers will not move it and the lever that will.

The discipline is the same one from the prefetch lesson: change one thing, watch ready and unacknowledged depth, consumer capacity, redelivery rate, and downstream latency, and confirm the limit moved where you expected. Adding consumers is cheap to try and easy to over-apply; the metrics tell you when you have crossed from “scaling” into “contending.”

06 Build

Prove single-stream FIFO, then reason about the pool

The deterministic, assertable invariant is that one channel’s publishes are enqueued in order and a single consumer receives them in that order. The checkpoint publishes ten numbered messages and drains them with one prefetch-limited consumer, asserting the received sequence equals the published sequence. In the comments it marks exactly where a second concurrent consumer would break completion order even though dequeue stays FIFO.

Fair worker-pool checkpointPython 3 · Pika 1.3.2

FIFO on one stream; distribution across many

python · install pika==1.3.2 and run against localhost
import uuid

import pika

QUEUE = f"lab.compete.fifo.{uuid.uuid4().hex[:10]}"
TOTAL = 10


def main():
    connection = pika.BlockingConnection(pika.ConnectionParameters("localhost"))
    channel = connection.channel()
    try:
        channel.queue_declare(
            queue=QUEUE,
            durable=True,
            arguments={"x-queue-type": "quorum"},
        )
        channel.confirm_delivery()

        # One channel publishes in order -> the queue enqueues in that order.
        for sequence in range(TOTAL):
            channel.basic_publish(
                exchange="",
                routing_key=QUEUE,
                body=str(sequence).encode(),
                properties=pika.BasicProperties(delivery_mode=2),
            )

        # A single prefetch-limited consumer dequeues in FIFO order.
        channel.basic_qos(prefetch_count=1, global_qos=False)
        received = []
        for method, _properties, body in channel.consume(
            QUEUE, inactivity_timeout=5
        ):
            if method is None:
                break
            received.append(int(body))
            channel.basic_ack(method.delivery_tag)
            if len(received) >= TOTAL:
                break

        # Invariant: dequeue order equals enqueue order for one stream.
        assert received == list(range(TOTAL)), received
        # With TWO concurrent consumers, delivery would still be dealt in order,
        # but completion/side-effect order would NOT be guaranteed. That needs a
        # single active consumer or key-partitioned queues (next lesson).
        print({"queue": QUEUE, "received": received, "fifo_preserved": True})
    finally:
        channel.cancel()
        channel.queue_delete(queue=QUEUE)
        connection.close()


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Run it and the assertion holds every time. Add a second consumer and process at different speeds, and the messages still leave the queue in order while your database rows land out of order — the exact gap between “FIFO delivery” and “ordered outcome” that the figures illustrate.

↗ Practice

Configure → Diagnose → Design

ConfigureAttach three equal-priority consumers to one quorum queue with a window of one each. Predict how six ready messages are distributed if consumer C stalls on its first message, and explain why the delivered counts are unlikely to be exactly two apiece.
DiagnoseThroughput on a single queue plateaus at sixteen consumers and stays flat at thirty-two. List the resources that could be binding (queue hot path, downstream, client, network, replication), the metric that confirms each, and why more consumers cannot move the plateau.
DesignOrders for the same customer must be applied in order, but you need parallelism across customers. Design a topology using key partitioning into several queues (each with a single active consumer) or super streams, and state how you handle redelivery without reordering.

Self-test

1. Among which consumers does round-robin actually distribute?

The active, eligible consumers of the relevant highest priority. A consumer with a full window or an unwritable connection is skipped, and lower-priority consumers are used only when all higher-priority ones are blocked.

2. Does round-robin guarantee each worker handles the same number of messages?

No. It distributes turns, not completed work. When processing speeds differ or a consumer is skipped, the delivered counts come out uneven.

3. Why does adding consumers to one queue eventually stop raising throughput?

A single queue replica has a single-core hot path, so its delivery rate plateaus. Client CPU, downstream latency, network, storage, acknowledgement, or replication cost can bind even earlier.

4. What actually happens with a very large or unlimited prefetch window?

A worker can accumulate a large private in-flight backlog, especially if consumers join or process unevenly. It does not deterministically dump the whole queue on one consumer; distribution still depends on eligibility, timing, priorities, and queue type.

5. Messages are dequeued in FIFO order — why do side effects still land out of order?

Multiple consumers process concurrently and finish at different times; redelivery and priorities also perturb order. In-order delivery does not imply in-order completion or acknowledgement.

6. How do you keep per-entity order while still scaling out?

Partition by a stable key into several queues, each with a single active consumer (or exactly one consumer) and non-concurrent processing, or use streams/super streams where offsets preserve order within a partition.
