Quorum Queues & the Raft Consensus Engine

A quorum queue is a queue that is a Raft group. That one sentence explains its durability, its failure behavior, and the single most misquoted detail in RabbitMQ: exactly when a publisher confirm is safe to trust. This lesson replaces “we set three replicas so we’re safe” with the precise rule — a majority commits, then RabbitMQ confirms.

Original study chapter. The scenarios, diagrams, code, and SVGs are written from scratch. Stable behavior targets open-source RabbitMQ 4.3, with the signed 4.3.3 maintenance release as the current patch overlay; runnable code uses Python 3 and Pika 1.3.2. This chapter contains three original figures, two interactive. Confirmed-message safety is conditional on a surviving majority; unconfirmed publishes carry no guarantee.
00 Failure first

“We set three replicas, so nothing can be lost”

A service switches its important queue to a quorum queue with three members and declares the reliability problem solved. Then a node hosting the queue leader dies mid-publish, and a batch of messages is simply gone. Nothing was misconfigured. The mistake was believing that having replicas is the guarantee. The guarantee is attached to a specific event — the publisher confirm — and only messages that were confirmed before the failure enjoy it.

The second misquote hides inside the first. People say “every replica acknowledges each write.” That is not how Raft works and not what RabbitMQ promises. Raft commits an entry once a majority of the group agrees; RabbitMQ then returns the publisher confirm. The other replicas catch up afterward, and their acknowledgement was never on the critical path. Understanding “majority, then confirm” is the difference between a design that survives a node loss and one that only appears to.

So this lesson builds the quorum queue from its engine outward: the Raft group, the leader that orders every change, the majority that commits it, the confirm that reports it, and the honest edges — election pauses, catch-up cost, sizing that helps versus sizing that just adds work, and the workloads a quorum queue is wrong for.

The core distinction

A quorum queue’s safety lives at the confirm. A message replicated to a majority and then confirmed should survive as long as a majority of members is not permanently lost. An unconfirmed publish has no such promise.

01 Mechanism

The queue is a Raft group: one leader, replicating followers ▸

A quorum queue is an always-durable, replicated queue implemented as its own Raft consensus group. One member is the leader; the others are followers. Every state change — an enqueue, an acknowledgement, a setting — is routed through the leader, appended to the Raft log, and replicated. This is why a quorum queue is fundamentally different from a classic queue: its correctness comes from a replicated log, not from a single owning process.

Because it is a Raft group, the commit rule is Raft’s. An entry is committed once a majority of the queue’s configured members have it. For a three-member queue the majority is two, so the leader plus one follower is enough to commit; the third follower’s copy arrives shortly after but was never required. The figure walks a single publish through send, replicate, majority commit, and only then the publisher confirm — then contrasts it with an unconfirmed publish that has no guarantee at all.

02 Invariant

The confirm marks the safety line; unconfirmed is ambiguous

Here is the precise contract. RabbitMQ issues a publisher confirm for a quorum-queue message only after that message has been replicated to a quorum and is considered safe. That confirm — a basic.ack back to the publisher — is not an application-visible acknowledgement from every replica; it is the broker’s statement that a majority committed the entry. Treat the confirm as the moment the message crosses from “maybe” to “durably replicated.”

From that contract follow the guarantees and the non-guarantees. A message that was publisher-confirmed should not be lost, provided a majority of the queue’s members does not become permanently unavailable. There is no documented safety for a publish that was never confirmed: if the connection or leader dropped before the confirm, the message may or may not be there. That is why a correct publisher retransmits every unresolved publish after recovery — and why retransmission can create duplicates, so consumers must be idempotent.

This is the whole reason publisher confirms and idempotent consumers travel together. The quorum queue makes the confirmed message durable against a minority failure; it does nothing about the messages you sent but never got a confirm for, and it does not make delivery exactly-once.

Interview follow-up

“Does a quorum queue guarantee no message loss?” Sharpen it: “It guarantees that a publisher-confirmed message survives as long as a majority of members survives. Unconfirmed publishes are ambiguous, so publishers retransmit and consumers dedupe. Confirmed-and-majority-safe is the promise, not exactly-once.”

03 Boundary

Leader loss is survivable, not free ▸

If the leader fails while a majority of members remains, a follower is elected leader and the queue continues. But “continues” is not “never paused.” During the leaderless interval — usually a few seconds — queue delivery and some client operations pause or delay. Confirms can be delayed or nacked, and unacknowledged deliveries can be requeued and later redelivered. So failover is transparent in the sense that you keep your data and your queue, not in the sense that latency is zero or that a message is processed exactly once.

Recovery of a returned member is likewise real work. A follower that was disconnected receives the Raft log entries it missed from the current leader. A short blip is cheap; a long outage can require substantial catch-up, and the returning member is not eligible to become leader until it has caught up. This is a genuine improvement over the old mirrored-queue synchronization model, but it is not cost-free — a member that fell far behind imposes replication load while it catches up.

The signed 4.3.3 maintenance release is relevant here: it fixes scenarios in which quorum-queue, Khepri, and other Raft leaders could optimistically commit new log entries. The release note establishes a correctness fix, not a documented message-loss incident, so the honest operational advice is “run the latest compatible 4.3 patch and read its notes,” not “older 4.3 loses data.”

04 Trade-off

Sizing that helps, sizing that just adds work

The default quorum-queue group size is three, which is the practical minimum for tolerating one member failure, and odd counts are strongly recommended. Failure tolerance is a property of each queue’s own member set, not of the broker: three members tolerate one loss, five tolerate two, two tolerate zero. A fourth voting member is the classic trap — four members still tolerate only one failure, so you have paid for extra replication without buying more resilience than three gave you.

More replicas are also not automatically faster. Quorum-queue throughput generally decreases as member count rises; performance tails off above five members, and more than seven is not recommended. Keep the two counts distinct in your head: the broker can have many nodes, while each queue has its own smaller member set — a five-node cluster commonly hosts three-member queues.

Membership is explicit. Adding a broker node does not move existing replicas onto it unless Continuous Membership Reconciliation (CMR) is enabled — and CMR is off by default, is not triggered by a transient node failure, and does not remove the need for deliberate member management. You grow, shrink, add, or remove members yourself; you set the initial group size at declaration or by configuration; and you keep member counts odd.

bash · membership and leaders are different operations, each verified
# Membership is EXPLICIT — adding a broker node does not move replicas onto it.
rabbitmq-queues add_member orders.work rabbit@node-d
rabbitmq-queues delete_member orders.work rabbit@node-a
# grow / shrink apply a membership change across many queues at once.
rabbitmq-queues grow rabbit@node-d all
rabbitmq-queues shrink rabbit@node-a
# Leaders are separate from membership: rebalance moves LEADERS, not members.
rabbitmq-queues rebalance quorum
# Always prove the members and the leader before and after a change:
rabbitmq-queues quorum_status orders.work
ini · rabbitmq.conf — the default initial group size for new quorum queues
# Default initial group size; a per-queue x-quorum-initial-group-size overrides it.
quorum_queue.initial_cluster_size = 3

Leader placement is a separate concern from membership. New members are chosen across nodes, including the declaring client’s node; the initial leader defaults to client-local, while a balanced locator considers existing leaders in smaller topologies and uses random selection at larger counts. For a Raft queue the locator is only a suggestion: election randomness means the suggested replica need not remain or become leader. When leaders bunch on one node, rabbitmq-queues rebalance quorum redistributes leaders — it does not change membership.

05 Boundary

Quorum queues are for important, long-lived queues — not everything

Consensus is not free, so quorum queues are meant for long-lived, important queues, not for every queue in the system. The 4.3 guidance gives soft thresholds, and they are guidance, not hard broker limits: review your topology once you have more than roughly 5,000 quorum queues; prefer streams when a single logical backlog exceeds roughly 5 million messages or needs large fan-out; and steer temporary, high-churn, or minimum-latency workloads away from quorum queues.

There are also firm functional exclusions. Quorum queues do not support non-durable, exclusive, or server-named use, and they do not participate in global QoS. They are disk- and consensus-heavy by design, so capacity and disk-latency testing remains part of adopting them — the replication that makes them safe is exactly what makes them demanding. Choosing a quorum queue is choosing to spend disk and network for durability, and that trade is worth making deliberately, queue by queue.

Production warning

Do not default every queue to quorum. Reserve them for data you cannot afford to lose, keep the member count odd and small, and treat the 5,000-queue and 5-million-message figures as review triggers, not guarantees or ceilings.

06 Build

Make the confirm the thing you assert on

The script declares a quorum queue with an explicit initial group size, puts the channel in confirm mode, and publishes with mandatory so an unroutable message would raise rather than vanish. In Pika’s blocking client, a publish in confirm mode raises if the broker nacks or returns, so reaching the line after each publish is the confirm. The assertions are on confirmed count and stored depth — the safety line the lesson defines.

Publisher-confirm boundary checkpointPython 3 · Pika 1.3.2

Publish in confirm mode against a quorum queue and assert the boundary

python · install pika==1.3.2 and run against localhost
import uuid

import pika

QUEUE = f"lab.l28.raft.{uuid.uuid4().hex[:10]}"
TOTAL = 10


def publish_confirmed(channel, queue, body):
    # In confirm mode Pika's blocking basic_publish RAISES on nack/return,
    # so returning normally means a MAJORITY committed the message.
    channel.basic_publish(
        exchange="",
        routing_key=queue,
        body=body,
        properties=pika.BasicProperties(delivery_mode=2),
        mandatory=True,
    )


connection = pika.BlockingConnection(pika.ConnectionParameters("localhost"))
channel = connection.channel()
try:
    # A quorum queue is its own Raft group. Ask for an initial group size of 3;
    # on a single dev node it starts with what is available and can be grown.
    channel.queue_declare(
        queue=QUEUE,
        durable=True,
        arguments={"x-queue-type": "quorum", "x-quorum-initial-group-size": 3},
    )
    channel.confirm_delivery()

    confirmed = 0
    for index in range(TOTAL):
        publish_confirmed(channel, QUEUE, f"event-{index}".encode())
        confirmed += 1  # reached only because the publish was confirmed

    # The confirm is the safety boundary: an UNCONFIRMED publish has no promise,
    # so a real publisher retransmits unresolved sends and dedupes downstream.
    assert confirmed == TOTAL
    ready = channel.queue_declare(queue=QUEUE, passive=True).method.message_count
    assert ready == TOTAL
    print({"confirmed": confirmed, "ready": ready})
finally:
    channel.queue_delete(queue=QUEUE)
    connection.close()

Everything the queue promises is anchored to those confirmed publishes. Simulate a leader loss in a real cluster and you will see the queue survive on its majority, deliveries pause briefly, and any unconfirmed sends remain ambiguous — exactly the boundary the asserts encode.

Self-test

1. When does RabbitMQ return a publisher confirm for a quorum-queue message?

After the message has been replicated to a quorum (a majority of the queue’s configured members) and is considered safe. The confirm is the broker’s statement that a majority committed the entry — not an acknowledgement from every replica.

2. Is a message on a three-member quorum queue guaranteed never to be lost?

Only if it was publisher-confirmed and a majority of members does not become permanently unavailable. Unconfirmed publishes have no safety guarantee, so publishers retransmit and consumers must be idempotent.

3. What actually happens when the queue leader fails?

If a majority remains, a follower is elected leader. Delivery and some operations pause for usually a few seconds, confirms can be delayed or nacked, and unacknowledged deliveries can be requeued. It is survivable, not instant or exactly-once.

4. Why is a four-member quorum queue usually a mistake?

Four members require a majority of three and still tolerate only one failure — the same as three — while adding replication cost and reducing throughput. Odd counts (3, 5) are recommended.

5. Does adding a broker node move existing quorum replicas onto it?

No. Membership is explicit unless Continuous Membership Reconciliation is enabled, and CMR is off by default and not triggered by a transient failure. Use add/delete/grow/shrink, and remember rebalance quorum moves leaders, not members.

6. Are 5,000 quorum queues or a 5-million-message backlog hard limits?

No — they are workload guidance and review triggers. Beyond them, review topology or move large backlogs and fan-outs to streams. Quorum queues also cannot be non-durable, exclusive, server-named, or use global QoS.
